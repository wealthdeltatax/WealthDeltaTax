# 174r: Growth effects of replacing phase-one taxes

Accessed 2026-11-26. Evidence review only; no causal forecast or model change.

Status: block 2 complete. External literature reviewed; local held-literature extraction and checker not run.

## 1. Summary

- The direction of the OECD tax-and-growth literature is relatively consistent at a broad level: corporate income taxes are among the most harmful taxes for long-run growth; recurrent taxes on immovable property are among the least harmful. This is a ranking across tax structures, not an estimate of the GDP growth dividend from this particular six-tax switch.
- The six taxes do not share one growth effect. Corporation tax can raise the user cost of capital and deter marginal investment. Business rates tax property occupation/improvements and can distort where firms locate or invest. Stamp duty reduces transactions and mobility. Council tax is a recurrent property tax, but the UK system's stale valuations and band structure are separate distortions. CGT creates lock-in and asset-allocation effects, but the sign and size depend on rate, basis, realisation and inflation. IHT effects on entrepreneurship and saving are ambiguous and evidence is mixed.
- The WDT is not equivalent to a standard annual net wealth tax. It taxes annual net-wealth change under a particular base, schedule, exemption and loss-refund arrangement. Its growth effect depends on whether it taxes normal returns, how it treats unrealised gains and losses, liquidity, debt, pensions, business assets, and the use of proceeds. Existing wealth-tax evidence cannot be transferred without a mapping.
- No reviewed empirical paper identifies the GDP-growth effect of switching exactly one percentage point of UK GDP from this six-tax bundle to this WDT. The conversion requested by the brief is therefore a labelled model sensitivity, not an empirical estimate.
- Candidate axis: annual change in real GDP growth per 1 percentage point of GDP revenue switched, low −0.05 percentage points, zero 0, high +0.05 percentage points. These are deliberately symmetric scenario settings, not confidence bounds or literature-derived coefficients. They imply about −1.0%, 0%, or +1.0% in the GDP level after 20 years for a sustained 1%-of-GDP switch, before general-equilibrium feedback.

## 2. What is being compared

The relevant experiment is a revenue-neutral replacement: government raises the same amount of revenue, but the tax instruments and bases change. That is different from:
- a tax cut financed by borrowing or lower spending;
- a tax-rate change that alters total revenue;
- a comparison of statutory rates without effective tax rates, allowances or behavioural responses;
- a partial-equilibrium estimate of investment, transactions, employment or taxable income;
- a long-run cross-country regression ranking tax categories.

The model parameter should be defined as a growth-rate increment per unit of revenue switched, not as an investment elasticity or a change in the statutory rate. Let x be the fraction of GDP revenue switched and let delta_g be the annual real GDP growth-rate change in percentage points. The axis is delta_g = beta*x. beta is not identified by the literature reviewed here.

The six taxes also differ in incidence and base:
- corporation tax: profits and marginal investment returns;
- council tax: recurrent domestic property taxation;
- business rates: recurrent non-domestic property taxation, based on rateable values;
- CGT: realised capital gains, with timing and basis effects;
- SDLT/stamp duties: transactions, including property and possibly securities depending on the definition in the WDT project;
- IHT: transfers at death and lifetime gifts under the relevant rules.

The WDT is a tax on wealth change, not simply a recurrent property tax or a conventional net wealth tax. Its treatment of unrealised gains, losses, debt and business assets matters.

## 3. Evidence by tax and mechanism

### 3.1 Corporation tax

The OECD's 2008 cross-country growth regressions rank corporate income taxes as the most growth-damaging broad tax category in their sample, with recurrent immovable property taxes at the least harmful end. OECD's 2025 Economic Outlook review summarises a large empirical literature finding a negative relationship between corporate income tax and business investment: higher CIT lowers after-tax returns and can make marginal projects unviable; liquidity constraints can amplify the effect.

This is the strongest directional case for a positive growth effect from replacing corporation tax with a less distortionary base. It does not quantify the GDP growth gain from a revenue-neutral switch. Effective rates, capital allowances, financing treatment, multinational profit shifting, market power and the destination of the replacement revenue all matter.

### 3.2 Council tax

Recurrent immovable property taxes generally perform relatively well in cross-country growth rankings because the tax base is relatively immobile and the tax does not directly tax the marginal return to new investment in the same way as corporation tax. That broad result does not imply the present UK council tax is well designed. IFS identifies stale valuations and regressivity; revaluation and rate restructuring can change distribution and local revenue allocation without changing aggregate revenue.

A well-designed recurrent property tax is a plausible low-distortion comparator. Council tax should not be assigned the same negative growth effect as transaction taxes merely because both are property-related. The exact UK effect is not identified.

### 3.3 Business rates

UK business rates are a tax on non-domestic property occupation and, because liabilities can rise when property values or improvements rise, can discourage investment in business premises. OECD's 2024 UK Economic Survey explicitly notes that property taxes can weigh on investment that would improve property values, particularly for smaller firms.

Micro evidence is mixed on what a rate cut achieves. Lockwood, Simmler and Tam (2026) find that targeted small-business and retail reliefs reduce commercial vacancies, with effects varying by targeting and local context. Matikonis and Gobey (2024), studying generic small-business property-tax reductions and productivity, find that generic reductions are mostly associated with lower productivity when significant; they warn that tax cuts are not automatically a productivity policy. These results are not contradictory: occupancy, investment and measured productivity are different outcomes, and relief design differs.

Business rates are plausibly more distortionary than a neutral land-value tax or a broad recurrent property tax, but no study reviewed here supplies a GDP growth coefficient per pound of rates removed.

### 3.4 Stamp duty

The evidence that transaction taxes suppress transactions is unusually direct. Hilber and Lyytikainen (2012) exploit a discontinuity in the UK SDLT schedule: a two-percentage-point increase in the marginal rate reduced homeowners' propensity to move by around 40%, with the effect mainly on short-distance and non-job-related moves. HMRC's 2021 commercial SDLT study estimates a 1% increase in the effective tax rate is associated with an 11.7% change in commercial transactions under its preferred regression; it reports residential semi-elasticities of 5–7%.

These are transaction elasticities, not GDP elasticities. Lower stamp duty can improve housing and commercial-property reallocation and labour mobility, but the fraction of suppressed moves that are productive job matches is not established. A revenue-neutral replacement could also change house prices through capitalisation.

### 3.5 Capital gains tax

CGT can create a lock-in effect: owners delay realisation to defer tax, potentially preventing assets from moving to higher-value uses and distorting portfolio choice. The size depends on the effective rate, basis rules, reliefs, holding period and whether gains are taxed on accrual or realisation. A WDT charged annually on wealth change may reduce the realisation lock-in, but it can instead tax gains before liquidity is available and tax normal returns. Symmetric loss refunds and a high exemption change the comparison again.

The reviewed sources do not identify a robust UK aggregate GDP-growth coefficient for CGT replacement. Do not assume all CGT revenue is collected from a pure rent or that removing it automatically raises productive investment.

### 3.6 Inheritance tax

IHT can affect bequest timing, gifts, saving, business succession and avoidance. The sign of its effect on entrepreneurship is not settled. A 2025 cross-country study by Zhang et al. using GEM data and policy reforms in 55 countries reports higher inheritance taxes associated with higher entrepreneurship index scores, with tax cuts associated with declines. This is not a UK causal estimate and entrepreneurship indices are not GDP growth. It challenges the simple claim that lower IHT necessarily encourages enterprise.

IHT replacement by WDT could reduce death-related timing distortions, but a WDT may tax wealth accumulation more continuously and affect incentives to save or invest. There is no direct evidence for the net growth effect of the proposed WDT design.

### 3.7 Wealth-based taxation and the WDT

Evidence on conventional net wealth taxes is mixed and highly design-dependent. Zucman (2020), using Danish administrative wealth records and the 1989 reform, finds reduced-form behavioural responses among the wealthiest and a sizable long-run elasticity of taxable wealth with respect to the net-of-tax return in a calibrated life-cycle model. That establishes a behavioural margin, not an aggregate GDP-growth loss of a known size.

Røed et al. (2022) exploit Norwegian wealth-tax reforms and find a positive causal relationship between the household wealth-tax level and subsequent employment growth in closely held firms. The authors discuss incentives to shift wealth into untaxed intangible and human-capital investment. This result directly cautions against assuming a wealth tax must reduce employment or investment in every design.

Schnellenbach's 2012 survey concludes that efficiency arguments for net wealth taxes are theoretically ambiguous and that some empirical evidence points to lower GDP growth. This is a broad survey, not a causal estimate of the WDT.

WDT-specific mechanisms remain unmeasured:
- annual taxation of accrued/unrealised changes may reduce realisation lock-in but increase liquidity constraints;
- symmetric loss refunds may reduce downside risk relative to asymmetric taxes, but the actual refund cap and timing matter;
- taxing normal returns may discourage capital formation, while taxing economic rents may have smaller effects;
- exemption thresholds, valuation rules and avoidance responses alter the effective base;
- replacing corporation tax and transaction taxes may improve investment allocation, while replacing council tax may offer less growth gain;
- public use of receipts matters. A revenue-neutral tax switch is not growth-neutral if spending composition changes.

## 4. Low, zero and high scenario-axis candidates

No paper above estimates beta for this reform. The following is a deliberately narrow sensitivity range for the model, not an empirical confidence interval.

Definition: beta = annual real GDP growth-rate change (percentage points per year) for each 1 percentage point of GDP in revenue switched from the six phase-one taxes to the WDT. Hold the switch constant over the stated horizon. These settings are scenario assumptions.

| Candidate | beta | GDP-level difference after 20 years, sustained 1% GDP switch | Class | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| Low | −0.05 pp/year | about −1.0% | scenario | WDT behavioural/liquidity and normal-return costs dominate any reduced distortion |
| Zero | 0.00 pp/year | 0.0% | scenario | Revenue-neutral reallocation has no net long-run growth effect |
| High | +0.05 pp/year | about +1.0% | scenario | Removal of corporation-tax, business-rates and stamp-duty distortions dominates WDT costs |

For a switch of x percentage points of GDP, multiply beta by x as a first-order scenario rule. The level illustration uses compound growth: (1 + beta/100)^20 − 1. It is arithmetic, not a model simulation. Do not apply a one-off 1% GDP switch as if it were a permanent annual growth dividend; the scenario assumes the revenue share remains switched and the growth-rate difference persists.

The range is intentionally not wider because the evidence does not calibrate a large productivity dividend. A more extreme positive setting would be a policy hypothesis, not a research finding. The high case is not a forecast, and the zero case is not a claim that tax structure is irrelevant.

## 6. Weaknesses, transferability and unset parameters

1. **Wrong dependent variable.** Much evidence is about investment, mobility, vacancy, employment, taxable wealth or entrepreneurship, not GDP per capita growth.
2. **Revenue neutrality.** Tax cuts are often studied without a matched replacement tax. The WDT switch must hold revenue and spending constant to isolate tax structure.
3. **Different institutional settings.** OECD panels, Denmark and Norway are not the UK; tax bases and institutions differ.
4. **Tax design heterogeneity.** Statutory rates are not effective marginal rates. Allowances, exemptions, thresholds and valuation rules can dominate.
5. **WDT is novel.** Annual wealth-change taxation with symmetric loss treatment is not directly represented by conventional recurrent wealth-tax studies.
6. **Public spending.** Growth effects depend on how receipts are spent or used to retire other taxes. A tax-only parameter cannot capture spending multipliers or public investment.
7. **Transition.** A gradual phase-in, grandfathering, threshold indexation and tax replacement sequence can change both short- and long-run effects.
8. **Capitalisation and incidence.** Removing property taxes can be capitalised into land/property values; who gains is not the same as the change in productive capacity.

Unset: the revenue share actually switched in each year; the mix of the six taxes by year; whether WDT taxes normal returns or rents; the treatment of unrealised gains and liquidity; whether business rates are replaced by a land-value-like base or simply removed; and whether the SWF's tax-replacement spending rule changes aggregate demand or public investment.

## 7. Open rulings

- Whether beta is applied to each tax replacement separately or only to the combined phase-one switch.
- Whether the scenario axis is permanent (annual growth-rate effect) or transitional (one-off level effect). The table above is permanent.
- Whether the author wants the symmetric ±0.05 pp/year range retained or a wider stress test.
- Whether the model should distinguish corporation tax, business rates and stamp duty from the less clearly distortionary council tax, CGT and IHT components.
- Whether any future empirical work should model the WDT itself or keep beta as a labelled macro sensitivity.
