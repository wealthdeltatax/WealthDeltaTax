## Answer

No close analogue among the major sovereign funds reviewed uses a simple fixed maximum stock with every pound above it automatically transferred to a designated tax-replacement programme. The nearest mechanisms are (i) a reserve target with residual balances invested elsewhere (Botswana's liquidity portfolio/Pula Fund), (ii) a target reserve ratio with reduced assessments or discretionary dividends above thresholds (US FDIC), and (iii) a rule that limits annual withdrawals rather than fund size (Alaska, Norway). Chile's pension reserve has a contribution ceiling/size condition, but its stabilisation fund does not have a stated maximum in the sources reviewed. Alberta's present growth target is aspirational, not a cap; its history shows how withdrawals can hollow out a fund without formal closure.

### What is weak or conflicting

- “Cap” is often used loosely. POMV (Alaska) caps the annual draw, not the stock. Norway's 3% is a long-run spending guideline, not a maximum return or balance. FDIC's 2% is a designated reserve goal, with no legal ceiling.
- Chile's Ministry FAQ is a useful primary explanation but is not the law. Its PRF size condition is displayed as “900 UF”; the unit multiplier and current legal wording need checking against the consolidated Fiscal Responsibility Law.
- Botswana's six-month benchmark is reviewed periodically; it is a liquidity reserve target, not an invariant statutory ceiling.
- I did not find a primary-source rule for an excess-stock transfer in Alberta, Alaska, Norway or New Zealand. A stock cap plus automatic excess allocation is not the normal architecture in these comparators.

## Relevance to our design

For the **cap** and **excess release**, the comparison set spans three distinct mechanisms: a fixed balance cap with automatic sweep (not found in these examples); a minimum reserve target with surplus relief through pricing or discretionary dividends (FDIC); and a liquid-reserve floor with residual assets assigned to a long-term portfolio (Botswana). Alaska's 5% POMV and Norway's 3% expected-return guideline constrain annual flows rather than cap the stock. These should not be treated as equivalent. The repo's excess-above-both-reserves-to-tax-replacement rule is therefore a relatively unusual explicit allocation rule, not a direct copy of a named fund precedent.
