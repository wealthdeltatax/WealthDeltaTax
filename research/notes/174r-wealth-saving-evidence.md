# 174r: Wealth to GDP and saving by wealth class

Accessed 2026-11-26. Evidence ranges for the SWF redesign; no forecast and no model changes.

Status: block 1 complete (questions 1 and 2). External-source evidence; held-pack extraction and local reproduction not run.

## 1. Summary

- Long-run UK evidence supports a large, variable and valuation-sensitive household/private wealth stock, not a mechanical rule that wealth must outgrow GDP. Piketty and Zucman find a European private wealth/national-income U-shape: roughly 200–300% in 1970 rising to 400–600% in 2010, with UK historical values returning towards 600–700% around 1870–1910. This is wealth/national income, not household net wealth/GDP; do not splice it into ONS national-balance-sheet series.
- ONS national-balance-sheet household wealth/GDP rose from 319% in 1995 to 453% in 2007, fell to 392% in 2008, then recovered to 541% in 2021 (as summarised in Measuring the Economy, ch. 10, figure 10.6). That is a +222 percentage-point rise over 26 years, but it contains large asset-price and denominator effects and the post-2020 survey evidence is weaker.
- Latest ONS national balance sheet estimates put household net worth at £10.8tn in 2025, comprising £4.5tn non-produced assets (mainly land), £2.1tn produced assets (mainly dwellings) and £4.3tn net financial worth. The rounded components do not sum exactly because of rounding and accounting identities. NBS is a different source and concept from WAS.
- WAS household composition in 2020–22: 40% net property, 35% private pensions, 14% net financial and 10% physical wealth. This is a distribution-survey measure, not a direct split into WDT-taxable returns. WAS accreditation was suspended in June 2025; treat post-2020 wealth-distribution estimates with caution.
- No held UK source identified here gives a robust saving rate by wealth decile. Existing project review 145r reaches the same conclusion. Evidence by income quintile or liquid savings supports, at most, a directional gradient; it cannot calibrate a wealth-ranked saving gradient.
- Scenario-axis candidates for annual log drift of household wealth/GDP: low −0.5%, central-for-illustration 0%, high +0.5%. These are modest sensitivity settings, not estimated forecasts. Historical observations include larger multi-decade movements, but much of that is cyclical valuation and changing national-account definitions.

**Sources and conventions.** Piketty and Zucman (2014), Capital is Back: Wealth-Income Ratios in Rich Countries 1700–2010, QJE 129(3), 1255–1310, abstract and figures II/IV; Measuring the Economy, chapter 10, figure 10.6 (citing ONS National Balance Sheet Table 11); ONS, The national balance sheet and capital stocks, preliminary estimates, UK: 2026, section 4. The Piketty-Zucman historical series is private wealth relative to national income. The ONS series is household wealth relative to GDP. They are complementary historical context, not one continuous series.

The ONS NBS household stock includes produced assets, non-produced assets (principally land) and net financial worth. It is not simply a stock of assets generating taxable financial returns. Land value is an asset-price/capitalisation measure, while private pension wealth and pension entitlements depend on accounting boundaries. A comparison with WDT's taxable-return base needs a separate decomposition.

The WAS component shares sum to 99% because of rounding. The survey covers Great Britain, whereas NBS is UK-wide. Do not treat WAS private pension wealth as equivalent to NBS financial net worth: the accounting treatment and coverage differ.

OSR suspended accredited status for WAS in June 2025 after declining response rates and lack of investment materially weakened quality. OSR judged that the statistics were no longer of sufficient value or quality to meet user needs; ONS says the suspension applies from Round 8 (2020–22) onwards. Round 9 quality was expected to deteriorate further. The 2020–22 component shares are useful descriptive context, but not a clean calibration target.

### 2.3 What the historical ratio says, and does not say

The observed U-shape is consistent with the mechanism in Piketty and Zucman: for a given net saving rate, lower long-run income growth raises the wealth/income ratio (their illustration: net saving of 10% implies roughly 300% at 3% growth and 600% at 1.5% growth). But their paper also attributes the post-war fall and later recovery to changes in asset prices and capital policies. The UK household series from 1995 to 2021 likewise has a large valuation component: the 2007–08 fall is too rapid to interpret as a change in household saving behaviour alone.

The 2025 NBS release illustrates the distinction between stock growth and financial wealth growth: household net worth rose by £146.1bn in 2025; produced assets rose 3.3% to £2.1tn, non-produced assets fell 0.9% to £4.5tn, and household financial net worth rose 2.9% to £4.3tn. Land and dwelling revaluations matter materially. A wealth/GDP trend is therefore not a direct measure of saving or productive capital accumulation.

## 3. Long-run wealth/GDP drift: scenario-axis candidates

The model needs a scenario range, not a forecast. Use annual log drift in the wealth/GDP ratio so the setting is scale-independent:

| Axis candidate | Annual log drift | Approximate 50-year ratio multiplier | Evidence class | Rationale |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| Low | −0.5% | 0.78 | scenario | Allows sustained relative decline; the post-war decline and asset repricing show this is possible |
| Central-for-illustration | 0.0% | 1.00 | scenario | Neutral baseline; avoids extrapolating a valuation-driven historical rise |
| High | +0.5% | 1.28 | scenario | Allows wealth to outgrow GDP moderately; consistent with some long-run re-accumulation evidence but below the full 1970–2010 rise |

These are not confidence intervals and are not estimated from a regression. The 1970–2010 Piketty-Zucman rise is materially larger than +0.5% per year if read as a smooth trend, but it includes the post-war reversal in asset prices and changing capitalisation. The 1995–2021 ONS ratio rise is also larger, but it is not a stable long-run drift estimate. A further stress case could use ±1% per year, but it is not included in the three core candidates because it compounds to 0.61 or 1.65 over 50 years and is a deliberately severe sensitivity.

**Important model distinction:** this ratio axis describes private household wealth relative to GDP. It does not imply the SWF reserve stock should compound at the same rate. Under the author's capped-fund decision (25 November), the fund tracks its reserve targets; excess is used for tax replacement. The relevant bridge is the path of WDT receipts and the reserve-sizing rule, not an assumed uncapped SWF asset-growth process.

## 4. Saving and accumulation by wealth class

### 4.1 Evidence boundary

No held UK source identified in this review reports a clean saving rate by household wealth decile, measured consistently as net saving divided by income or wealth. Existing project review 145r reports the same gap after checking WAS, ONS saving-ratio material and related sources. Its central conclusion remains valid: UK evidence by income group or liquid-savings change is not a wealth-ranked saving-rate estimate.

The closest UK distributional evidence in 145r is Resolution Foundation pandemic-era liquid-savings evidence by **income quintile**: typical liquid savings rose by £80 for the lowest income quintile and £4,200 for the highest between 2019–20 and 2021–22 (RF, Before the Fall, pp. 7 and 32). This is a pound change in liquid savings over an exceptional period, not a saving rate; income rank is not wealth rank. It supports a directional income gradient only.

The same 145r note records a Norwegian net-active-saving shape (about 6–8% of disposable income above roughly the 20th percentile, near zero around the 15th percentile) as a foreign proxy, not a UK estimate. Its top-1% gross saving figure of about 35% includes capital gains and was explicitly excluded from calibration. See 145r for the full evidence table, shape construction and challenges.

### 4.2 Aggregate saving ratio is not a wealth gradient

ONS Blue Book series DG5H reports the household saving ratio (non-seasonally adjusted annual values) from 1963. Selected observations: 4.8% in 1963; 12.7% in 1981; 5.2% in 1999; 7.8% in 2007; 10.8% in 2009; 5.7% in 2019; 16.5% in 2020; 5.0% in 2022; 9.6% in 2025. This demonstrates substantial aggregate time variation, including a pandemic spike, but says nothing about allocation of saving across wealth classes. It also differs from the adjusted saving ratio used in some ONS household-arm inputs; match definitions before comparing.

### 4.3 Defensible spread across classes

| Candidate | Evidence status | What it can support | What it cannot support |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flat saving by wealth line | scenario baseline | No assumed wealth gradient; preserves aggregate saving | Not a claim that actual saving is flat |
| Mild bottom-versus-rest gradient | proxy for direction; magnitude scenario | Directional sensitivity informed by income-ranked liquid-savings differences and Norwegian banded evidence | UK wealth-decile rate or top-tail rate |
| Steep gradient increasing to the top | scenario only | Stress test for concentration and top-line accumulation | Empirical estimate; top-1% saving magnitude |

The 145r shapes are already constructed with income-weighted mean-zero offsets, so aggregate saving remains equal to the flat case at each tested aggregate saving value. Its mild step is 7 percentage points between the bottom two lines and the rest; its steep case adds a linear rise towards the top. These are deliberately labelled proxy for direction and scenario for magnitude. They should be carried forward as sensitivities, not described as empirical household saving rates.

## 5. Implications for the household arm

1. Keep aggregate saving ratio and wealth gradient as separate assumptions. The ONS aggregate ratio is a time series; it does not identify distribution.
2. Do not use pandemic-era liquid savings in pounds to calibrate a permanent wealth-ranked saving rate.
3. Do not use the Norwegian top-1% gross saving rate including capital gains as a UK net-saving estimate.
4. Separate active saving, capital gains, pension accrual, debt repayment and revaluation. Wealth changes are not synonymous with saving.
5. The WDT itself can alter net accumulation if tax is paid from wealth or income. That is a model mechanism, not a reason to infer the pre-tax saving gradient from post-tax wealth changes.
6. For wealth/GDP, the three candidate drifts above are sensitivity settings only. The empirical record supports substantial long-run movement but not a stable point estimate for the future.
