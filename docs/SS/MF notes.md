Yes. But I don't think there is **one existing moral system that maps cleanly onto MF**. It looks more like a hybrid of several traditions, with one particularly strong family resemblance.

If I were trying to locate it philosophically, I'd investigate these in roughly this order:

### 1. Republicanism — probably the closest political philosophy

Not Republicanism in the American party sense. **Civic republicanism**, particularly Philip Pettit's version.

Pettit's central idea is **freedom as non-domination**: you're not fully free merely because nobody currently interferes with you; you're unfree if someone possesses arbitrary power over you. ([OUP Academic][1])

That maps surprisingly well onto MF.

The MF intuition seems to be:

> It is perfectly legitimate for someone to possess wealth and exercise substantial economic power. The problem arises when that power becomes sufficiently unilateral that others are effectively dependent upon the owner's continued discretion.

That is very close to the republican concern with **arbitrary power**, without requiring economic equality.

And Pettit's republicanism explicitly distinguishes **structural equality** from **material equality**. You can have unequal resources while attempting to ensure citizens possess equal political standing and freedom from domination. ([OUP Academic][2])

That's almost exactly the distinction MF seems to be making.

There is an especially interesting connection here:

**WDT's taxation isn't primarily taking power away from ownership. It is intended to prevent ownership from becoming a source of unaccountable power.**

That's a distinctly republican way of thinking.

---

### 2. Scanlonian contractualism — very strong fit for the reciprocity argument

T. M. Scanlon's contractualism asks whether principles governing society can be **reasonably rejected by the people subject to them**.

Its core idea is that moral principles should be justifiable to others as free individuals who have their own interests. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][3])

This maps onto several MF ideas:

* prospective participation;
* reciprocal obligations;
* treating people as individual moral subjects;
* justification rather than simple aggregation;
* asking whether both sides can reasonably accept the institutional arrangement.

The particularly interesting overlap is that Scanlon isn't simply asking:

> “Does this produce the greatest total welfare?”

He's asking:

> **“Can I justify this principle to the individual who is being asked to live under it?”**

That is extremely close to the moral instinct behind MF's insistence that the wealthy person must also have a legitimate claim against the system.

The difference is important, though.

**Scanlon is much less committed to reciprocity between social groups than MF appears to be.**

MF seems to say:

> “You participate in the institutional system, therefore you owe something to it and it owes something to you.”

Scanlon's framework is more fundamentally about **justification between persons**.

---

### 3. Rawls — surprisingly close, but also surprisingly different

At first glance you'd expect MF to be very Rawlsian because both are concerned with **social cooperation** and reciprocal advantage.

Rawls explicitly starts from the idea that social cooperation is necessary and that citizens care about how its benefits and burdens are distributed. His theory treats **equality-based reciprocity** as fundamental. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][4])

But there is a major divergence.

Rawls essentially asks:

> **What distribution of social and economic goods can free and equal citizens accept?**

MF seems to ask:

> **What institutional relationship between unequal citizens can remain mutually legitimate?**

That's a meaningful difference.

Rawls starts with a much stronger presumption in favour of equality and then permits inequalities when they satisfy his principles.

MF starts with:

> **inequality is morally permissible.**

The moral problem is instead **whether the relationship between unequal parties remains reciprocal and politically sustainable**.

So I'd call MF **Rawls-adjacent rather than Rawlsian**.

---

### 4. Classical social-contract theory

There is also a very obvious Hobbes/Locke/Rousseau family resemblance, although MF doesn't neatly resemble any one of them.

The underlying structure is:

**individuals → common institutional order → reciprocal obligations → legitimacy**

The interesting difference is that MF seems to treat the social contract as something much more **ongoing**.

It's not:

> “We once agreed to establish government.”

It's closer to:

> **“The legitimacy of the institutional arrangement has to be continuously reproduced because both sides remain participants in it.”**

That makes MF's contract almost **dynamic**.

And this explains why the WDT's symmetric gain/loss treatment is morally important. The state isn't merely taking payment for services. It is entering into a continuing reciprocal relationship with the individual.

---

### 5. Adam Smith's moral philosophy

This one is less obvious but I think potentially very fruitful.

Smith's *Theory of Moral Sentiments* isn't fundamentally about maximising aggregate welfare. It develops the idea of the **impartial spectator**: moral judgement involves imaginatively occupying a standpoint from which one's conduct can be assessed in relation to others. ([OUP Academic][5])

There is something Smithian about MF's insistence that the system must be defensible **from both sides**.

Imagine reversing the identities:

> “Suppose I were extraordinarily wealthy and had accumulated my assets under the old rules.”

versus:

> “Suppose I were an ordinary citizen with no meaningful wealth but whose political system was increasingly shaped by the owners of enormous fortunes.”

The author appears to think a legitimate institution has to survive **both perspectives**.

That's much closer to impartiality than to class morality.

---

## But there's another tradition I think is particularly revealing: **classical republican civic virtue**

This is where the author gets more unusual.

The implicit ethical model seems to be:

> **A free society requires citizens who have both rights and a stake in maintaining the institutions that secure those rights.**

That is much older than modern welfare-state thinking.

The republican tradition historically worried about **dependence**: if citizens become economically dependent upon powerful individuals, their formal political freedom can become hollow.

Modern republican theory makes this explicit. Pettit argues that non-domination requires institutions preventing arbitrary power, not merely institutions preventing actual interference. ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy][6])

This gives you an interesting interpretation of the WDT:

**WDT isn't fundamentally an equality machine.**

It is potentially a **dependency-control mechanism**.

That is a much more philosophically interesting proposition.

---

## And I think there's a fifth element that doesn't fit neatly into those systems

### Mutualism / reciprocity

MF has an unusually strong intuition that **rights and obligations should be symmetrical**.

Something like:

> If you retain the upside, you accept the downside.

> If society protects private accumulation, society receives a claim on the resulting wealth increase.

> If society claims a share of appreciation, the individual receives protection against corresponding losses.

> If citizens are expected to support the institutional order, they should possess a meaningful stake in that order.

This isn't standard utilitarianism.

It isn't straightforward egalitarianism.

It isn't libertarianism.

It isn't conventional Rawlsianism.

It is much closer to an ethical principle of:

$$
\boxed{\text{Participation creates reciprocal obligation}}
$$

And **that may actually be the distinctive ethical core of the author's system.**

---

## So I'd draw the intellectual map like this

| Tradition                  | MF resemblance      | Main difference                                             |
| -------------------------- | ------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Civic republicanism**    | **Very strong**     | MF gives wealth ownership much more room                    |
| **Scanlon contractualism** | **Very strong**     | MF emphasises institutional reciprocity more                |
| **Social contract theory** | **Strong**          | MF treats the contract as continuously reproduced           |
| **Rawls**                  | **Moderate–strong** | MF doesn't make equality the baseline                       |
| **Adam Smith**             | **Moderate**        | MF is more institutional/systematic                         |
| **Libertarianism**         | **Partial**         | MF rejects one-sided property claims                        |
| **Utilitarianism**         | **Partial**         | Welfare is important but not the sole moral criterion       |
| **Egalitarianism**         | **Weak–moderate**   | Equality isn't treated as intrinsically valuable            |
| **Marxism**                | **Weak**            | MF explicitly accepts private capital and unequal ownership |

### The really interesting synthesis

I'd describe the moral architecture as something like:

> **Liberal individualism + republican non-domination + contractualist justification + reciprocal obligation + pragmatic consequentialism.**

But there's an even more concise way of putting it:

> **People may legitimately be unequal in wealth, but they may not legitimately become unrelated in obligation.**

That seems to capture something unusually characteristic of MF.

And if you're thinking about this academically, I think **Pettit is the person I'd investigate hardest**. The overlap between *non-domination*, *institutional design*, *political legitimacy*, *material independence*, and *unequal resources without requiring material equality* is potentially substantial. Pettit's work explicitly connects non-domination to political legitimacy and democratic influence, so it could provide a much stronger philosophical literature base for the argument you're developing around WDT than simply citing generic “wealth inequality” literature.
