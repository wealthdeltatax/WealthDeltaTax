I would insert it **after the current purchasing-power calculation and before the cascade-threshold discussion**.

The logic becomes much stronger if the paper moves from **direct household effect → behavioural response → economy-wide feedback → welfare consequences**.

# 3.6 Demand Expansion and Economic Reallocation

I'd make this section explicitly a **hypothesis**, rather than presenting the downstream effects as established results.

The opening should essentially say:

> The purchasing-power calculation above describes the immediate household effect of tax displacement. It does not describe the subsequent response of households, firms or labour markets. If households spend a substantial proportion of their additional purchasing power, the resulting increase in demand should itself alter the composition of economic activity.

Then introduce the key distinction:

### 3.6.1 Increased Household Consumption

Here you take the £11,516 and say:

**That is not necessarily the end of the welfare effect.**

The economy doesn't hold the extra £11,516 in a box.

Some gets:

* consumed
* saved
* invested
* used to repay debt

And consumption becomes someone else's revenue.

I'd particularly highlight **services** because this is where your hypothesis gets interesting.

A household that previously couldn't justify:

* eating out regularly
* hiring a cleaner
* taking lessons
* going on additional holidays
* paying for repairs rather than DIY
* fitness/recreation
* cultural activities
* personal services

now can.

That creates demand for businesses that didn't previously have enough customers.

---

# 3.6.2 Labour Reallocation and Occupational Choice

This is where I'd introduce your genuinely novel hypothesis.

Something like:

> Increased purchasing power may also change the opportunity cost of labour. A worker who currently requires a high wage to maintain their standard of living has a strong financial incentive to remain in higher-paid employment. If essential consumption becomes less expensive and disposable income increases, the income required to maintain a given standard of living falls. The worker may consequently become willing to accept lower monetary earnings in exchange for autonomy, shorter hours, occupational preference or entrepreneurial opportunity.

Then make an important distinction:

**This is not necessarily a reduction in labour supply.**

It could be a **reallocation of labour supply**.

Someone might work:

> £50k corporate job → £32k independent business

rather than:

> £50k corporate job → no work.

That's important because otherwise someone will immediately attack the argument with "higher welfare reduces labour supply."

Your hypothesis is more specific.

---

# 3.6.3 Entry into Marginal and Previously Unprofitable Sectors

This should probably be the most provocative subsection.

Your claim is essentially:

> Some activities are not economically viable because there aren't enough consumers willing or able to pay for them, while others are unattractive because the opportunity cost of entering them is too high.

WDT potentially attacks **both constraints simultaneously**.

### Demand constraint

More people can afford the product/service.

### Labour constraint

More people can afford to work in the sector even if the initial income is relatively low.

That could produce a feedback:

**higher household purchasing power**

→ more customers

→ higher expected revenue

→ more viable businesses

→ more occupational entry

→ greater supply

→ greater variety / competition

→ potentially lower prices and higher consumer surplus.

This is a much stronger economic story than simply "people buy more stuff."

---

# 3.6.4 Returns to Private Capital

Then bring in your point about WDT taxpayers.

I would **not** title this "Benefits to WDT Taxpayers."

I'd call it:

### **3.6.4 Returns to Private Capital**

Then say:

> This mechanism creates a potentially important second-order effect for WDT taxpayers themselves. WDT taxes increases in private net worth, but WDT taxpayers continue to own private capital. If increased household purchasing power generates additional demand and business formation, owners of productive private capital may capture part of the resulting increase in economic activity.

Then distinguish:

**Potential beneficiaries**

* businesses serving newly expanded consumption
* commercial property
* productive firms
* infrastructure suppliers
* technology
* logistics
* entertainment
* consumer services

from:

**Potential losers**

* incumbent firms whose rents are competed away
* scarcity-based assets
* sectors displaced by SWF investment
* businesses facing new entrants
* capital dependent on artificially high household costs

This is important because it prevents the section becoming:

> WDT taxes wealth, but secretly makes the wealthy richer.

Your actual proposition is much more interesting:

> **WDT may tax wealth accumulation while simultaneously expanding the economic environment in which productive capital operates.**

Those aren't contradictory.

---

# 3.6.5 Distributional Ambiguity

I think this subsection is worth having specifically because you're trying to make the paper robust against hostile reading.

Say explicitly:

> The direction of the aggregate effect does not determine its distribution.

Some capital owners gain enormously.

Some lose.

Some workers move from high-paying employment to lower-paying but preferred work.

Some workers remain in high-paying employment and simply consume more.

Some businesses experience increased demand.

Others face new competition.

Therefore the claim isn't:

> everyone becomes richer.

It's:

> **the equilibrium allocation of labour, consumption and capital may become substantially different.**

That is much harder to knock down with a single counterexample.

# Then the cascade section becomes stronger

Your current cascade-threshold argument actually gets **better** if this comes before it.

The sequence becomes:

### Direct effect

> Household purchasing power rises.

↓

### First-order behavioural response

> Households consume more / save more / repay debt.

↓

### Second-order market response

> Businesses experience additional demand.

↓

### Labour response

> People become more willing to enter lower-paid, autonomous or entrepreneurial activities.

↓

### Production response

> More firms and sectors become viable.

↓

### Capital response

> Returns change across different forms of private capital.

↓

### Household resilience

> Higher financial margins make ordinary economic shocks less likely to become crises.

That's a **much more complete LDW model**.

---

## One thing I would *not* do

I wouldn't put numerical estimates on these new effects yet.

Your existing £11,516 calculation is already sufficiently striking. If you then say:

> "and therefore GDP rises 8%, employment rises 3%, entrepreneurial entry rises 14%..."

without a model, critics can simply attack the assumptions.

Instead, make this section deliberately say:

**Here is a mechanism that follows from the purchasing-power result. It is currently a hypothesis requiring modelling.**

That actually fits the character of your WDT research very well.

You could even finish the section with a boxed research question:

> **Research question:** Does a large increase in household purchasing power, combined with reduced dependence on high-wage employment, increase business formation and occupational diversification sufficiently to raise aggregate productivity and returns to productive private capital?

And then your next section asks a different question:

> **What happens to welfare when increased financial margins reduce the frequency with which ordinary shocks cross household crisis thresholds?**

That gives LDW a very clean architecture:

**tax incidence → purchasing power → demand → labour allocation → production → capital returns → resilience → welfare.**

I think that's the right place for this material.
