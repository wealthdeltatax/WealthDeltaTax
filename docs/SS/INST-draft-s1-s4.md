# Abstract {.unnumbered .unlisted}

The academic literature on institutional fitness and democratic resilience treats fiscal architecture as a fixed parameter — one governance system or another operates within whatever tax instruments happen to exist. This paper argues that assumption is wrong in a specific and consequential way.

A fiscal instrument now exists whose stable implementation is constitutionally compatible only with democratic governance, and which unlocks a pool of private capital that authoritarian systems are structurally excluded from accessing. If the Wealth Delta Tax's properties hold under implementation, democracies may have been competing against authoritarian systems while leaving their most significant potential fiscal advantage entirely unmobilised — not through choice, but through the prior absence of a workable mechanism.

The paper does not demonstrate that advantage. It establishes the preconditions for it, identifies the structural mechanisms through which it would operate, and proposes the empirical programme required to confirm or falsify it. Three epistemic tiers are maintained throughout. Tier A claims are demonstrated in the WDT mechanism papers or in established external literature and are not re-argued here. Tier B claims are logical implications of Tier A results when applied to the institutional competition framework — derived here, but not empirically verified. Tier C claims are evolutionary and diffusion hypotheses that follow from Tier B if the mechanism performs as designed; they are explicitly labelled as research programme propositions rather than conclusions.

The central argument proceeds in four stages. The first establishes the empirical baseline: authoritarian governance is not a historical aberration being steadily displaced but the persistent equilibrium, with the post-1989 democratic wave now substantially reversed and approximately 70% of humanity currently living under some form of autocratic rule. The second establishes the capital pool: roughly $570 trillion in global household wealth is distributed across governance tiers in ways that make approximately $286 trillion structurally inaccessible to the states nominally governing it — not because taxation is impossible, but because every prior wealth tax mechanism has failed through three identifiable and independently sufficient political failure modes. The third establishes the structural exclusion: the Wealth Delta Tax is the first proposed mechanism that plausibly satisfies all four conditions required for a stable large-scale wealth tax, and satisfying all four simultaneously requires a specific governance architecture that authoritarian systems cannot stably maintain without generating endogenous pressure toward the democratic accountability that makes the mechanism self-sustaining. The fourth identifies three competitive advantages that flow from this if Phase One is implemented and demonstrated, and examines what happens when the argument is recognised by governments operating under competitive pressure.

The paper's honest conclusion is that the WDT may be the missing tool that democracies have not yet realised they are missing. Whether that is correct can only be established through implementation.

# Glossary {.unnumbered .unlisted}

**Authoritarian endurance advantage:** The demonstrated capacity of authoritarian governance systems to impose costs on populations without electoral accountability, sustaining lower welfare thresholds during external pressure or internal hardship than democratic systems can politically maintain. A genuine competitive property of authoritarian governance, not a rhetorical concession.

**Compounding head start:** The accumulated advantage of the first WDT-adopting jurisdiction across all dimensions — valuation infrastructure, adviser ecosystem knowledge, sovereign wealth fund reserves, behavioural evidence base — that cannot be recovered by later movers at equivalent cost.

**Credible commitment problem:** The structural difficulty faced by any government that needs to make long-term fiscal promises binding on its successors. In the WDT context: the symmetric refund guarantee is only cooperatively credible if it cannot be suspended at governance discretion; this requirement creates a constitutional constraint incompatible with concentrated executive authority.

**Developmental authoritarianism:** A governance arrangement in which a concentrated ruling authority deliberately maintains separation between economic and political power — permitting private wealth accumulation while preventing its conversion into political faction — in exchange for rapid industrialisation and growth. The canonical cases are South Korea under Park Chung-hee, Singapore under Lee Kuan Yew, Taiwan under the KMT, and China through the Deng-to-Hu period. Distinct from predatory authoritarianism, which fuses economic and political power in the governing coalition.

**Endogenous institutional pressure:** The mechanism by which an authoritarian state attempting to satisfy the WDT's four operating conditions generates internal pressure toward the democratic accountability structures those conditions ultimately require. The WDT does not impose democratisation externally; it creates conditions under which the institutional distance between "stable WDT equilibrium" and "democratic governance" narrows with each operating cycle.

**Epistemic tiers:** The three-level classification maintained throughout this paper distinguishing demonstrated results (Tier A), derived implications (Tier B), and hypothetical evolutionary claims (Tier C). Introduced in the Abstract; applied section by section.

**Fiscal architecture:** The specific combination of tax instruments, base definitions, rate structures, governance mechanisms, and constitutional protections that define how a state extracts revenue from economic activity. Treated in the institutional fitness literature as a fixed parameter of governance systems; this paper argues it is a variable that can alter the comparative fitness of those systems.

**Institutional fitness:** The degree to which a governance arrangement can repeatedly produce sufficient welfare, output, fiscal capacity, legitimacy, and adaptability to remain competitive over generational timescales. Distinguished from regime survival, which can be achieved through coercion without welfare maximisation.

**Labour dividend:** The constitutionally committed reduction in taxes on labour and consumption funded by WDT surplus revenue above the SWF floor targets. The mechanism through which the WDT's terminal goal — democratic flourishing — is actually pursued. Also the mechanism through which a majority constituency with a direct financial stake in the WDT's continuation is created.

**Memetic diffusion:** The spread of institutional arrangements across governance systems through recognition of competitive advantage rather than direct imposition or emulation. In this paper's usage: the process by which recognition of the WDT's competitive advantages would propagate adoption pressure across governance types, generating one of four response options (adopt, suppress, race, conflict) whose dynamics and costs differ systematically by governance tier.

**Option B:** The suppression strategy available to governments whose position depends on preventing WDT demonstration — operating through information control, counter-narrative, partial imitation that breaks the mechanism, and isolation from jurisdictions demonstrating the advantage. Distinguished from active opposition, which leaves the mechanism available for later adoption. China's August 2026 National Defence Mobilization Law revision is interpreted here as domestic Option B: pre-emptive suppression of the institutional conditions under which WDT adoption would become conceivable.

**Self-defeating dynamic:** The structural mechanism by which authoritarian attempts to implement comprehensive wealth taxation are undermined by the fact that the wealth-holding population is also the support coalition. Taxing the former at scale constitutes an existential threat to the latter. Distinct from the broader difficulty all governments face in taxing mobile capital: the self-defeating dynamic operates even when the wealth-holding population is immobile, because the threat is political rather than fiscal.

**Tier A / B / C:** See epistemic tiers.

**Two-constituency mechanism:** The novel political dynamic created by the full WDT architecture — with labour dividend — in which two non-overlapping constituencies with different reasons for demanding institutional accountability converge on the same governance architecture. The wealthy taxpayer constituency demands credible protection of the symmetric refund and valuation architecture. The majority constituency demands continued access to the labour tax reduction. Their convergence on identical institutional requirements — constitutional constraint on executive discretion, mandatory public transparency, independent oversight — is qualitatively different from the single-constituency representation pressure identified in the taxation→democratisation literature.

**Untaxed pool:** The approximately $286 trillion in household wealth held in Tier 4 and Tier 5 governance systems, which their governing states cannot efficiently access through cooperative mechanisms — not for technical reasons but for structural ones rooted in the self-defeating dynamic. A further $228 trillion in Tier 1 and Tier 2 systems has been similarly inaccessible to democratic states through prior wealth tax mechanisms, for the three political failure reasons identified in §4.

# 1. Introduction

This paper asks a question the existing literature on institutional fitness has not formulated: does a specific fiscal architecture exist that is constitutionally compatible only with democratic governance and that unlocks a resource pool unavailable to authoritarian systems?

The literature has not formulated it because, until the Wealth Delta Tax was proposed, the answer was clearly no. Every prior serious attempt at comprehensive wealth taxation — the Haig-Simons proposals, Shakow (1986), Auerbach (1991), the OECD wealth taxes operational between 1965 and 2017, the Saez-Zucman (2019) proposal — either failed in practice or remained theoretical. None provided a mechanism that survived the three political failure modes that have destroyed every implemented wealth tax in developed democracies. The resource pool was real and growing; the missing ingredient was a mechanism durable enough to access it.

The Wealth Delta Tax proposes such a mechanism. The companion paper series — WP, VAL, RATES, GOV, POL, BEHAV, ENV, FM, and associated appendices — establishes its mechanism design, derives its governance architecture from first principles, and demonstrates its fiscal properties across 73 historical starting conditions. Those papers are the evidentiary foundation for the Tier A claims made here. This paper does not replicate or extend them. Its contribution is to apply their results to a question of institutional competition that they were not designed to address, and to identify what that application implies — with explicit acknowledgement, at each step, of where the argument shifts from demonstrated to derived to hypothetical.

The paper's structure follows the logical sequence of the argument. §2 establishes the empirical baseline on governance system distribution: authoritarianism is the persistent equilibrium, not a historical aberration. §3 maps the untaxed capital pool against governance tiers: a growing quantity of private wealth sits in systems structurally unable to access it cooperatively. §4 explains why democratic systems have also failed to access equivalent wealth — through mechanism failure rather than political will failure. §5 specifies the four conditions any stable large-scale wealth tax must satisfy and establishes that the WDT is the first proposed mechanism that plausibly satisfies all four. §6 develops the three-stage structural exclusion argument: why predatory authoritarian systems face hard exclusion, why developmental authoritarian systems face endogenous institutional pressure when they attempt compliance, and why a WDT without the labour dividend is not a stable equilibrium in any governance context. §7 identifies the three competitive advantages that follow for democratic WDT adopters. §8 examines the diffusion dynamics when the argument is recognised under competitive pressure. §9 names the failure conditions that would falsify the thesis. §10 specifies the research programme.

A note on scope. This paper does not argue that democracy is morally superior to authoritarian governance. It does not predict that WDT-equipped democratic systems will outcompete authoritarian systems within any given timeframe. It makes no claim that history has a direction. Its argument is structural: that the WDT creates a specific competitive dynamic in which one governance system type can stably implement a mechanism that another cannot, and that this asymmetry constitutes an evolutionary pressure not previously identified in the institutional fitness literature.

# 2. The Persistent Dominance of Authoritarian Governance

The standard account of governance system history treats the post-1945 period as a transition — halting, non-linear, but fundamentally directional — from authoritarian toward democratic governance. The empirical record does not support this reading.

## 2.1 Governance Distribution, 1800 to 2025

The five-tier classification used in this paper follows the V-Dem Regimes of the World typology (v16, 2026) with one modification: V-Dem's electoral democracy category is divided to separate hybrid and developmental authoritarian states, where elections exist but are materially constrained, from electoral democracies where competitive elections are genuine and basic rights are protected. This produces:

Tier 1 — liberal democracy: free and fair elections, constitutional rule of law, independent judiciary, meaningful constraints on executive power. Tier 2 — electoral democracy: competitive elections, basic rights protected, weaker institutional checks on executive. Tier 3 — hybrid/developmental: elections present but substantially constrained; developmental authoritarian states — where economic and political power are deliberately separated — sit here alongside transitional regimes. Tier 4 — electoral autocracy: multiparty elections operated as legitimating facades over concentrated power. Tier 5 — closed autocracy: no meaningful competitive elections.

This classification is the methodological anchor for all governance-tier claims in the paper. The tier boundaries are sharp for analytical purposes; empirically, governance systems are continuous and individual-country assignments involve judgment. Order-of-magnitude population estimates draw on V-Dem (2026), Polity IV, and Our World in Data; pre-1900 figures are rougher. Colonial populations are assigned to Tier 5 as the most defensible default.

At seven snapshots across the period: in 1800, approximately 95% of humanity lived under closed autocracy. By 1900 this had fallen to roughly 74%, with the remainder distributed across nascent electoral systems under European constitutional monarchies and the early American republic — none of which would qualify as Tier 1 under contemporary criteria. The post-1945 period produced the first material democratic expansion: Tiers 1 and 2 combined grew from roughly 13% of world population in 1945 to approximately 35% at the democratic peak around 2005. By 2025, this had reversed. The V-Dem 2026 report finds Tier 4 — electoral autocracy — at approximately 3.8 billion people, 46% of humanity. Tier 1 liberal democracy has contracted from its peak of roughly 1.1 billion to approximately 600 million, around 7% of the world population. Combined Tiers 4 and 5 account for approximately 70% of humanity.

The democratic interlude of 1989 to 2005, treated in much of the political science literature as a "third wave" transition, looks on the longer view like a fluctuation around a persistent equilibrium rather than a directional trend. The Bäck and Hadenius (2008) finding of a J-shaped relationship between democracy and administrative capacity is useful here: highly authoritarian systems can have strong top-down administrative capacity; intermediate regimes — including the electoral autocracies now dominant — often perform poorly on both governance dimensions; highly democratic systems can develop strong administrative capacity through bottom-up societal control. The relevant competition is therefore not between simple democracy and simple authoritarianism, but between specific institutional configurations within each broad type, which is why the five-tier approach is more useful than a binary.

## 2.2 Authoritarian Competitive Advantages

The persistence of authoritarian governance is not an historical accident. Authoritarian systems possess two genuine competitive advantages over democratic ones; naming them clearly is a prerequisite for understanding where the WDT changes the competitive calculus.

The first is the capacity to impose costs without electoral accountability. Democratic systems must maintain majority welfare across the political cycle; the electorate withdraws support when welfare falls materially below expectation. Authoritarian systems face no equivalent constraint within the governing coalition's tolerance. This gives them the capacity to mobilise resources rapidly toward focal problems — military, infrastructure, crisis response — by imposing costs on populations that cannot organise effective resistance. The authoritarian advantage is not merely the willingness to impose costs; it is the structural absence of the electoral mechanism that makes cost-imposition politically terminal in democratic systems.

The second is informational: the stabilising effect of populations not knowing that a better alternative exists. Information suppression — control of media, restriction of assembly, management of external comparison — stabilises populations at welfare levels that would be politically unsustainable under free information flow. A population that cannot observe alternatives cannot demand them. This advantage is eroding as communication technology reduces the marginal cost of cross-border information flows, but the erosion is slow relative to political cycles and remains material.

These advantages are real, not rhetorical concessions. Any analysis that dismisses authoritarian competitiveness fails to explain the empirical distribution documented in §2.1.

## 2.3 The Authoritarian Trade-Off

Against those advantages, authoritarian systems accept a specific structural cost: they trade individual-instance longevity for system-type persistence. No authoritarian state has a particularly long continuous record. Chinese dynasties averaged roughly 275 years before collapse and replacement; the Soviet Union lasted 74 years; contemporary authoritarian states, operating in a more information-transparent environment than their predecessors, tend to be shorter-lived still. But authoritarianism as a system type re-emerges persistently after each collapse, because the conditions that produce it — concentrated power, weak horizontal accountability, limited civil society — are themselves persistent features of political economy rather than temporary failures.

Democratic systems theoretically trade short-run flexibility for long-run institutional resilience. Acemoglu, Naidu, Restrepo and Robinson (2019) find that transitions to democracy increase GDP per capita by approximately 20% in the long run, with the effects materialising primarily through improvements in state capacity, education, and health investment. This is consistent with democratic systems having long-run institutional advantages. It is not evidence that democratic systems are outcompeting authoritarian ones in the sense relevant to §2.1: the share of humanity living under democratic governance has not increased on the 200-year view, and has fallen sharply on the 20-year view.

The question this paper addresses is whether the WDT changes this competitive structure in ways the existing evidence cannot yet reflect, because the mechanism is new. The answer to that question cannot be established before Phase One implementation. The preceding analysis establishes only the baseline against which any such change would be measured: authoritarianism as the persistent equilibrium, with real competitive advantages, facing democratic systems whose long-run institutional advantages have not translated into population-level dominance.

# 3. The Untaxed Pool

The fiscal capacity of the state depends not only on the tax instruments it applies but on the wealth base those instruments can reach. As private wealth has grown substantially faster than state fiscal capacity over the past four decades, the gap between what states nominally govern and what they can efficiently tax has widened in both democratic and authoritarian systems. The WDT enters as a proposed solution to that gap. To understand its significance, the size and governance distribution of the gap must be established.

## 3.1 Global Private Wealth: Scale and Growth

Global household wealth stood at approximately $570 trillion in 2025, according to the McKinsey Global Wealth Watch (2026) — a figure consistent with the UBS Global Wealth Report (2025) and Credit Suisse's series through 2023. This represents a roughly fourfold increase from approximately $130 trillion in 2000, and a pace of wealth accumulation that has substantially outrun both global GDP growth and the growth of state tax revenue in virtually every jurisdiction.

The growth is not evenly distributed across the wealth spectrum. Saez and Zucman (2016, 2019) document, across multiple methodological approaches, that the share of wealth held by the top percentiles of the distribution has grown substantially in most developed economies since the 1980s. The specific figures are methodologically contested — Kopczuk (2015) and Bricker et al. (2016) argue that the capitalisation method overstates concentration — but the direction is accepted across methodological camps. Upper-tail wealth has grown faster than the median, faster than labour income, and faster than most asset classes accessible to ordinary households.

This matters for fiscal architecture because the WDT's revenue is heavily concentrated at the upper tail. RATES §5.1 notes that the Pareto-implied population above £3m is almost certainly lighter than the true distribution because the Wealth and Assets Survey systematically undersamples above that threshold, making revenue estimates from the cohort model a floor rather than a central estimate. The growing wealth concentration at the top of the distribution means the untaxed pool is both larger than historical records suggest and growing faster than the fiscal instruments currently applied to it.

The inversion is the first observation: Tier 1 liberal democracies hold 30% of global household wealth with 7% of the world's population. The second observation is that neither democratic nor authoritarian systems are currently mobilising the upper-tail portion of their wealth pool at scale. The OECD (2018) documents that twelve countries had recurrent net wealth taxes in 1990; by 2017 only four remained. # 4. Why Prior Mechanisms Failed

The pool documented in §3 has not been untaxed because taxation of large private wealth is technically impossible. Professional valuation of complex assets exists and functions in estate, bankruptcy, and acquisition contexts. Third-party reporting of financial assets is well established in the jurisdictions where the pool is concentrated. The fiscal infrastructure for comprehensive wealth taxation has been present in most developed economies for decades. The pool has been untaxed because every mechanism proposed to tax it has been destroyed by three structural failure modes, each independently sufficient to defeat a wealth tax, each operating through political rather than economic channels. Understanding these failure modes is the prerequisite for assessing whether the WDT is genuinely different from its predecessors or merely a more sophisticated variant of the same instrument.

## 4.1 The Empirical Record

By 1990, twelve OECD countries levied individual net wealth taxes: Austria, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Iceland, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Norway, Spain, Sweden, and Switzerland. By 2017, the number had fallen to four — France, Norway, Spain, and Switzerland — and France abolished its wealth tax (the ISF) in 2018, leaving three. The abolitions did not follow economic failure. Revenue was flowing at the time of abolition in most cases. Sweden's wealth tax raised approximately 15 billion kronor in 2006, the year before repeal. France's ISF collected consistently throughout its final decade while commanding 60 to 80% public support in survey research (Perret, 2021). Germany's Bundesverfassungsgericht struck down the wealth tax in 1997 for equal-treatment reasons derivable entirely from the tax's accumulated internal inconsistencies, not from revenue failure.

The political economy of abolition has been studied systematically by Clark, Gronwald, Guerrero Fernandez and Ramirez-Casillas (2022), who examine Austria, Sweden and France and find support for policy drift, business structural power, and the role of ideas in abolition — with limited evidence for simple median-voter explanations. Scheve and Stasavage (2012) identify, across nineteen countries from 1816 to 2000, that mass mobilisation during wartime rather than democratic suffrage is the primary driver of progressive inheritance taxation, which suggests that political conditions rather than fiscal efficiency determine when comprehensive wealth taxation is politically sustainable. The record is consistent: the mechanisms failed not because they raised too little revenue or faced insuperable administrative difficulty, but because they lacked the institutional architecture to survive the three political failure mechanisms identified below.

## 4.2 Legitimacy Collapse

The first failure mechanism is legitimacy collapse: the process by which a tax perceived as purely extractive — offering no visible reciprocal benefit to either its payers or the general public — loses political legitimacy even when popular in surveys.

The France case makes the mechanism legible. Support for the ISF was consistently above 60% in opinion surveys throughout its existence, reaching 80% in some readings (Perret, 2021). A tax with that level of popular support was abolished because survey support does not generate the organised political resistance necessary to defend an institution from concentrated opposition. The public approved of the wealth tax in the abstract. No member of the public had experienced anything concrete that they stood to lose when it was removed. The reciprocal relationship between the ISF and the public services it funded was real — revenue went to the general budget — but causally invisible. Individual taxpayers could not observe which hospital, school, or road their payment had funded. The broader public could not trace the connection between a levy on a small number of wealthy individuals and the services they themselves received.

This is a structural feature of the instrument, not a failure of communication. The asymmetry between visible extraction and invisible benefit is inherent in any wealth tax that directs revenue to general government expenditure. Making the reciprocal benefit visible requires either earmarking revenue to specific services legible to payers, or creating a different kind of reciprocal relationship — one in which the state's obligations to the taxpayer are as specific and enforceable as the taxpayer's obligation to the state.

## 4.3 Organised Opposition Advantage

The second failure mechanism is the organised opposition advantage: the structural asymmetry between a small, concentrated, well-resourced taxed population and a large, diffuse beneficiary population, which systematically advantages organised resistance over passive support. This is the Olson (1965) collective action asymmetry applied to wealth tax politics, and it operates with predictable regularity across the abolition cases.

The taxed population in a wealth tax — by design — is small, identifiable, and capable of bearing the cost of sustained political engagement. In most OECD wealth taxes, the taxable population numbered in the tens of thousands at most. Such a population can organise cheaply, fund sustained lobbying, retain legal and communications professionals, and maintain a political presence across multiple electoral cycles. The beneficiary population — in principle the majority of citizens, who receive the public services funded by wealth tax revenue — is too numerous, too dispersed, and too uncertain about their individual share of the benefit to mount equivalent organisation.

Clark et al. (2022) find that business structural power — the capacity of large firms and wealthy individuals to shift investment or residence credibly — operated as a disciplining mechanism on governments contemplating wealth tax expansion, independently of active lobbying. The disciplining mechanism does not require organised effort; the mere credibility of exit generates policy deference. In a context where the taxable population is also the population with the greatest structural economic power, this deference is reinforced by the fiscal incentive structure of the governments themselves, which depend on that population's investment and employment decisions.

## 4.4 Institutional Hollowing

The third failure mechanism is institutional hollowing: the gradual accumulation of individually defensible exemptions, valuation concessions, and enforcement gaps that converts a comprehensive wealth tax into one that reaches progressively less of the wealth it nominally taxes.

Sweden is the clearest case. Between 1991 and 2007, housing, listed shares, family business assets, and pension wealth were progressively treated differently, each modification responding to a specific lobbying campaign and each producing a plausible administrative justification. The cumulative effect was a wealth tax that nominally applied to net wealth above the threshold but effectively reached a shrinking share of total wealth held by the taxable population. By 2006 the mechanism had become administratively complex, politically indefensible as a matter of horizontal equity, and easily characterised as bearing more heavily on people who had not arranged their affairs cleverly than on genuinely large fortunes.

Germany illustrates the constitutional form. The Federal Constitutional Court's 1997 ruling struck down the wealth tax not because it was excessive but because its valuation methodology treated different asset classes inconsistently — producing equal-treatment violations derivable entirely from the accumulated ad hoc modifications of the preceding decades. The Court's ruling was the endpoint of an erosion process, not its cause.

The hollowing mechanism is politically distinct from the organised opposition advantage, though they interact. Organised opposition does not typically win by defeating a wealth tax in parliament. It wins by securing incremental concessions through administrative working groups, finance ministry consultations, and valuation methodology committees, where the political costs of resistance are high and the justifications for concession are always available. Each individual concession is defensible; the cumulative effect is invisible until the mechanism is already hollow. By the time a wealth tax has been exempted and complexified to the point of being a caricature of its stated purpose, the political cost of formal abolition is lower than the administrative cost of continuing, and repeal is presented as simplification rather than defeat.

## 4.5 The WDT's Structural Response

Each failure mechanism identifies a design requirement for any wealth tax that does not want to repeat the OECD pattern. Legitimacy collapse requires that the cooperative character of the arrangement be visible in real time, not inferred from diffuse public benefits. Organised opposition requires that a majority constituency exist with a concrete financial stake in the mechanism's continuation, not merely abstract preferences in surveys. Institutional hollowing requires constitutional protection against the specific channels — exemption creep, threshold drift, valuation discretion — through which OECD wealth taxes were eroded.

The WDT addresses all three at the design level rather than the political management level (POL §5). The symmetric refund mechanism makes the state's downside obligation specific, individually legible, and pre-funded: in a loss year, the refund is not a promise against future revenue but a drawdown from a ring-fenced reserve triggered automatically at the applicable marginal rate (GOV §4.2). The labour dividend creates a majority constituency with a direct financial stake — lower labour taxes, higher disposable income — whose continuation depends on the WDT's operation (POL §5.3). The ten enumerated structural clauses and three-chamber governance structure build constitutional resistance to hollowing into the mechanism: amending the most fundamental commitments triggers automatic Tier 2 review and a permanent rebalancing cost (GOV §5.2).

Whether those responses are sufficient is a Phase One empirical question (PHASE1 §5). What is established by the failure analysis is that they address the correct targets. The WDT is not a conventional wealth tax with better enforcement. It is a different kind of instrument whose architecture was derived from these failure mechanisms rather than grafted onto a conventional design. Whether that distinction is large enough to produce a different political outcome is the central question Phase One exists to answer.

The Saez and Zucman (2022) position is relevant here: they argue that historical European wealth tax failures resulted primarily from narrow bases attributable to exemptions, avoidance, and evasion, and that modern administrative capacity and base-broadening could make a wealth tax substantially more effective. The WDT agrees on the diagnosis but proposes a structural solution rather than an administrative one. The difference matters: stronger enforcement of a conventional mark-to-market wealth tax addresses the symptoms of the failure modes without removing their causes. A professional who produces a technically defensible but systematically conservative valuation is not deterred by tighter audit standards; a taxpayer who converts a liquid asset into an exempt structure is not deterred by better reporting requirements. The WDT's response is to change what the state needs to know — from the correct value of an asset to the consequences of whatever the taxpayer declared — so that the incentive to misdeclare changes rather than merely the probability of detection (VAL §1).
