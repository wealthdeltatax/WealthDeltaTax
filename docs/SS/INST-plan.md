## Section Structure

### §1 Abstract

Opens with the gap, not the solution.

The academic literature on institutional fitness and democratic resilience treats fiscal architecture as a fixed parameter — one governance system or another operates within whatever tax instruments happen to exist. This paper argues that assumption is wrong in a specific and consequential way: a fiscal instrument now exists whose stable implementation is constitutionally compatible only with democratic governance, and which unlocks a pool of private capital that authoritarian systems are structurally excluded from accessing. If the Wealth Delta Tax's properties hold under implementation, democracies may have been competing against authoritarian systems while leaving their most significant potential fiscal advantage entirely unmobilised — not through choice, but through the prior absence of a workable mechanism.

The paper does not demonstrate that advantage. It establishes the preconditions for it, identifies the structural mechanisms through which it would operate, and proposes the empirical programme that could confirm or falsify it. The central claim is a hypothesis, not a conclusion.

### §2 The Persistent Dominance of Authoritarian Governance

**Purpose:** Establish the empirical baseline. Authoritarianism is not a historical aberration being steadily displaced — it is the persistent equilibrium. The democratic interlude of 1945–2005 looks, on the longer view, like a fluctuation rather than a trend.

**Content:**

Five-tier governance classification (V-Dem Regimes of the World as the methodological anchor):
- Tier 1: Liberal democracy — free elections, rule of law, constitutional executive constraint
- Tier 2: Electoral democracy — competitive elections, basic rights, weaker institutional checks
- Tier 3: Hybrid / developmental — elections present but contested; developmental authoritarian states sit here
- Tier 4: Electoral autocracy — multiparty facade over concentrated power
- Tier 5: Closed autocracy — no meaningful competitive elections

Population by tier, seven snapshots 1800–2025. Key observations:

Tiers 1 and 2 combined never exceeded ~35% of world population even at the 2005 democratic peak. The V-Dem 2026 report places current Tier 4 population at approximately 3.8 billion — 46% of humanity — with Tier 1 having contracted from its ~1.1 billion peak to roughly 600 million. The post-1989 democratic wave is now substantially reversed.

Authoritarian systems have two genuine competitive advantages. First: they can impose costs on populations without electoral accountability — a demonstrated capacity to run populations at lower welfare thresholds during external pressure or internal hardship. Second: the "not knowing a better alternative exists" equilibrium. Information suppression stabilises populations at welfare levels that would be politically unsustainable under free information flow. Both advantages are real and should not be minimised.

Authoritarian systems trade individual instance longevity for system-type persistence. No authoritarian state has a particularly long continuous record — dynasties collapse, regimes fall — but authoritarianism as a system type re-emerges persistently. Democratic systems theoretically trade short-run flexibility for long-run institutional resilience; the empirical record does not yet cleanly confirm this.

The J-shaped relationship between democracy and administrative capacity (Bäck & Hadenius, 2008) supports the five-tier approach over a crude binary: highly authoritarian systems can have strong top-down capacity; intermediate regimes often perform poorly; highly democratic systems can develop strong administrative capacity through bottom-up societal control. The relevant competition is not simply democracy versus authoritarianism but between specific institutional configurations within each broad type.

**External literature:** V-Dem Regimes of the World (v16, 2026); Polity IV; Bäck & Hadenius (2008); Acemoglu, Naidu, Restrepo & Robinson (2019) — democratisation increases GDP per capita by roughly 20% in the long run, but the relationship is heterogeneous.

**Tier A:** Empirical population data and external literature findings.
**Tier B:** Characterisation of authoritarian competitive advantages as structural rather than contingent.

### §3 The Untaxed Pool: Private Wealth Concentration by Governance Tier

**Purpose:** Establish the size and governance distribution of the capital pool at the centre of the argument. Make the evolutionary pressure visible as a quantity.

**Content:**

Total global household wealth: approximately $570 trillion in 2025 (McKinsey Global Wealth Watch, 2026), a fourfold increase from roughly $130 trillion in 2000. The growth rate of private wealth has materially outpaced the growth of state fiscal capacity in both democratic and authoritarian systems.

Distribution by governance tier (order-of-magnitude estimates, V-Dem 2025/2026 classifications):

| Tier | Population | Wealth | Wealth/capita | WDT accessible? |
|------|-----------|--------|---------------|-----------------|
| T1 Liberal democracy | ~600M / 7% | ~$171T / 30% | ~$285k | Yes — fully |
| T2 Electoral democracy | ~1.0B / 12% | ~$57T / 10% | ~$57k | Partial |
| T3 Hybrid / developmental | ~800M / 10% | ~$57T / 10% | ~$71k | Fiscal yes, governance no |
| T4 Electoral autocracy | ~3.8B / 46% | ~$200T / 35% | ~$53k | No — self-defeating |
| T5 Closed autocracy | ~2.0B / 24% | ~$86T / 15% | ~$43k | No — structurally blocked |

The inversion: Tier 1 holds 30% of global wealth with 7% of population. Neither system type is currently mobilising the upper-tail portion of its wealth pool at scale. The OECD (2018) documents that twelve countries had recurrent net wealth taxes in 1990; by 2017 only four remained. The pool has been untaxed not because taxation is structurally impossible but because every prior mechanism failed through the three political failure modes documented by Perret (2021) and analysed in (POL §3).

The crossover: WDT-inaccessible wealth (T4+T5, ~$286T) overtook WDT-accessible wealth (T1+T2, ~$228T) around 2013, driven primarily by Chinese private wealth accumulation. The gap is widening. This is the evolutionary pressure the paper analyses.

### §4 Why Prior Mechanisms Failed: The Three Structural Failure Modes

**Purpose:** Establish why the untaxed pool has remained untaxed in democratic systems. The failure is mechanism failure, not political will failure. This section is load-bearing for the paper's central claim that the WDT is qualitatively different from its predecessors.

**Content:**

Three structural failure mechanisms (POL §3), each independently sufficient to destroy a wealth tax:

**Legitimacy collapse.** Taxpayers experienced the tax as extraction without visible reciprocity. France: 60–80% survey support at abolition throughout its final decade (Perret, 2021). A tax with majority popular support was abolished because support in surveys does not translate into political resistance when the benefit being defended is abstract and causally invisible.

**Organised opposition advantage.** The taxed population — small, concentrated, wealthy, professionally advised — mobilised political resistance at a cost far below what the diffuse majority could organise to defend. Clark, Gronwald, Guerrero Fernandez & Ramirez-Casillas (2022) find support for policy drift, business structural power, and the role of ideas in wealth-tax abolition across Austria, Sweden and France, with limited evidence for a simple median-voter explanation. This is not political failure — it is the Olson (1965) collective action asymmetry operating as predicted.

**Institutional hollowing.** Individually defensible exemptions accumulated invisibly into structural erosion. Sweden: housing, listed shares, family firms, and pensions progressively carved out. Germany: Federal Constitutional Court struck down the wealth tax in 1997 because accumulated valuation inconsistencies could not survive the equal-treatment test. Each concession was defensible; the cumulative effect was invisible until complete.

The WDT's design addresses all three failure modes — not as political management but at the structural level (POL §5). Whether those design responses are sufficient is a Phase One question (PHASE1 §5). That they address the correct targets is established by the failure analysis.

Critical distinction from Saez & Zucman (2022): their argument is that historical European wealth taxes failed due to narrow bases from exemptions, avoidance and evasion, and that modern design and enforcement could make a wealth tax substantially more effective. The WDT agrees on the diagnosis but proposes a different solution: rather than improving enforcement of conventional mark-to-market valuation, it changes the question the state needs to answer — from "what is this asset worth?" to "what did the taxpayer declare it was worth?" (VAL §1). This is a structural departure from Saez & Zucman's approach, not a minor refinement.

**External literature:** Perret (2021); Clark et al. (2022); Olson (1965); OECD (2018); Saez & Zucman (2022).
**WDT references:** POL §3.1–§3.6; POL §5.1–§5.6; VAL §1; PHASE1 §5.

### §5 Four Conditions for a Stable Large-Scale Wealth Tax

**Purpose:** Establish precisely what any stable wealth tax mechanism requires. These are not WDT-specific design choices — they are necessary conditions derivable from the failure analysis in §4 and from first principles. The section does not yet argue that authoritarian systems cannot satisfy them; that argument comes in §6.

**Content:**

**Condition 1: No accurate external valuation required.**
The mechanism must not depend on the state certifying asset values it cannot directly observe. If it does, the state enters a sustained technical competition with well-resourced opponents it will reliably lose. The WDT satisfies this through self-declaration routes: a declared value becomes the legally operative basis; tax liability flows from subsequent deltas (VAL §1). This condition is governance-neutral — authoritarian states could in principle implement self-declaration architecture.

**Condition 2: Cooperation must be the path of least resistance.**
The mechanism must make voluntary compliance more attractive than avoidance across a wide range of declaration strategies, without requiring enforcement of individual asset values. The WDT satisfies this through the tolerant zone (α ≈ 0.8–1.5 producing outcomes close to honest declaration), the Route C must-transfer rule, and the refund-protection asymmetry (VAL §7.1). This condition is partially governance-neutral: it requires taxpayers to believe the refund guarantee is credible, which creates a dependency on Condition 3.

**Condition 3: The refund guarantee must be constitutionally protected against regime discretion.**
The symmetric refund is the mechanism's cooperative character made concrete. A government that can suspend the refund when convenient — which any government without binding constitutional constraints can do — destroys the cooperative mechanism (GOV §4.2). Rational taxpayers anticipating potential suspension will declare conservatively or restructure, approaching the failure modes of conventional wealth taxes regardless of the architecture's design quality.

This is the condition Andersson (2023) makes most relevant. His finding that authoritarian systems with institutional oversight can develop greater fiscal capacity raises the question directly: does Condition 3 require *democratic* accountability, or merely *credible commitment*? The answer the paper develops in §6 is that the specific combination of commitments the WDT requires — symmetric refund protection, mandatory public transparency, independent valuation bodies, lottery-constituted oversight chamber — is collectively compatible only with democratic accountability structures, even if each element individually might be reproduced through elite bargaining.

**Condition 4: The taxable population must not be the governing coalition.**
The WDT's support coalition is the majority — DR's 50% derived from the anti-collusion guarantee, filled by monthly lottery from the general population (GOV §3.2). This separation — taxable population as fiscal subject, majority population as governing coalition — is what makes the three failure modes addressable. Where the wealthy minority and the governing coalition overlap substantially, legitimacy collapse, organised opposition, and institutional hollowing operate with structural inevitability rather than merely political probability.

The "Regime Security and Taxation in Autocracies" literature (European Journal of Political Economy, 2021) finds that autocratic rulers choose tax structures partly according to whether elites or the mass population constitute the main threat to regime security. This directly confirms Condition 4's mechanism: where elites are the threat, elite taxation is relaxed; where popular rebellion is the concern, taxation can be shifted toward elites. Authoritarian governments do not merely face difficulty taxing wealthy elites — they are structurally incentivised to avoid doing so when those elites constitute the support coalition.

**WDT references:** VAL §1, §7.1; GOV §3.2, §4.2; BEHAV §8.11; POL §3; RATES §6.
**External literature:** Andersson (2023); European Journal of Political Economy (2021); Gilley (2017).

### §6 The Authoritarian Exclusion: Structural, Dynamic, and Empirically Grounded

**Purpose:** The load-bearing structural argument, now developed in three stages. First: why authoritarian systems face structural exclusion from the WDT. Second: why an authoritarian state attempting to satisfy the conditions would generate endogenous institutional pressure toward democratic accountability. Third: why the WDT without its full architecture — specifically without the labour dividend — is not a stable authoritarian equilibrium. China's trajectory as the empirical illustration throughout.

**Content:**

#### §6.1 Predatory and Electoral Autocracy: Hard Structural Exclusion

In predatory authoritarian states (Tier 5) and most electoral autocracies (Tier 4), the wealthy population and the governing coalition are fused. The European Journal of Political Economy (2021) analysis of 105 autocracies confirms the mechanism: where elites constitute the regime-security threat, elite taxation is structurally constrained. This is not a political difficulty of the kind democratic wealth taxes face — it is a regime-survival constraint. Taxing the support coalition at scale is regime suicide.

The result is not that authoritarian states cannot extract from wealthy populations. They can and do, through selective confiscation, forced "donations," and opacity-based informal extraction. But these are predatory mechanisms that destroy cooperative compliance rather than generating it. They are the opposite of what Condition 2 requires. A population that knows extraction can be arbitrary does not declare honestly — it hides assets, restructures ownership, and exits. The behavioural response to arbitrary extraction is exactly the avoidance pattern the WDT's cooperative architecture was designed to prevent.

#### §6.2 Developmental Authoritarianism: The Genuinely Interesting Case

The canonical developmental authoritarian states — Park Chung-hee's Korea, Lee Kuan Yew's Singapore, Taiwan under the KMT, China through the Deng-to-Hu period — maintained deliberate separation of economic and political power. The wealthy class could accumulate freely but could not convert wealth into political faction. This partially satisfies Condition 4 and creates more WDT-compatible conditions than predatory authoritarianism.

But Andersson (2023) provides the critical framework here. His finding that authoritarian systems with institutional oversight can develop greater fiscal capacity raises the question: could a sufficiently institutionally sophisticated developmental authoritarian state satisfy Condition 3 through elite bargaining and constitutional guarantees, without becoming democratic in the relevant sense?

The answer the paper develops is: yes, initially — but by doing so it begins changing its own political equilibrium. Gilley (2017) on China and Zhang (2017) on fiscal sociology both identify the representation dilemma: stronger, more comprehensive taxation generates demands for political accountability that authoritarian systems struggle to contain. The WDT amplifies this pressure through two distinct channels, creating constituencies for accountability that conventional taxation does not.

**The first constituency — wealthy taxpayers demanding institutional protection:**
WDT requires credible commitment that the state will honour the symmetric refund guarantee, will not arbitrarily reassess declared values, and will maintain the valuation architecture that makes the cooperative equilibrium work. Satisfying these requirements means constraining executive discretion over fiscal architecture in ways that are institutionally transformative. Schiller (2018) finds that institutionalised political parties increase governments' ability to make credible fiscal contracts with wealthy taxpayers — the implication being that credible fiscal commitment is itself an institutional investment with political consequences. An authoritarian state building the institutions required for WDT credibility is building the institutional infrastructure of constraint.

**The second constituency — the majority demanding continued access to the labour dividend:**
This is where the WDT differs categorically from conventional taxation, and where the INST paper's most novel contribution lies. The existing taxation→representation literature (Kato & Tanaka, 2019; World Development, 2013; Ross, 2004) operates on a single constituency: the taxed population demands representation because it resents the burden. The WDT creates a second, distinct constituency through the labour dividend. Once labour taxation falls materially in consequence of WDT revenue, the working majority acquires a direct financial interest in the continued operation of the WDT fiscal architecture. Their representation pressure is not "we resent being taxed" but "we benefit from this mechanism and want a say in how it is governed."

The two constituencies have different reasons for demanding institutional accountability, they target the same governance architecture, and — crucially — they are politically non-overlapping. An authoritarian state facing simultaneous institutional demands from wealthy taxpayers wanting predictable rules and from the working majority wanting to protect their tax reduction faces a qualitatively different political pressure than one that merely has to manage elite fiscal resistance. The WDT is potentially the first fiscal instrument that generates cross-class coalitions for institutional accountability rather than the single-class resistance that conventional taxation produces.

Kato & Tanaka (2019) warn that taxation doesn't mechanically cause democracy: contemporary autocracies frequently adopt modern taxation before democratisation, meaning taxation isn't sufficient to cause democratic transition. This caveat applies here. The WDT does not guarantee democratic transition. What it does is make the institutional conditions for sustainable operation increasingly resemble democratic accountability, such that the gap between "stable WDT equilibrium" and "democratic governance" narrows with each passing assessment cycle.

#### §6.3 The WDT Without the Labour Dividend: Not a Stable Authoritarian Equilibrium

An authoritarian state might attempt to access the capital pool while withholding the labour dividend — directing WDT net revenue to state priorities rather than majority welfare. This appears to offer the fiscal advantage without the political pressure of a majority constituency.

It does not work, for two independent reasons.

First, it fails to generate the second constituency. Without the dividend, the majority never acquires a financial stake in the mechanism's continuation. The only political force preserving the WDT is the wealthy taxpayer class — small, concentrated, and in an authoritarian context, susceptible to elite bargaining rather than institutional protection. The three POL failure mechanisms — legitimacy collapse, organised opposition, institutional hollowing — operate with full force. The WDT without its defending majority constituency is a conventional wealth tax with better architecture but the same political vulnerability. The Gilley (2017) finding is directly applicable: China's authoritarian resilience rests partly on its ability to provide services while limiting the political consequences of taxation. A WDT that generates revenue but withholds the visible majority benefit breaks the same implicit bargain.

Second, and more fundamentally, it degrades cooperative compliance over time. The cooperative mechanism the WDT depends on requires taxpayers to perceive the arrangement as reciprocal — the state participates in downside as well as upside, the refund is real and visible, the architecture serves the taxable population as well as extracting from it. A state directing revenue entirely away from majority welfare has broken the reciprocity signal. The membrane (BEHAV §6) degrades. Declaration ratios drift toward the lower end of the tolerant zone or below it. Avoidance shapes (BEHAV §8) multiply. The mechanism approaches conventional wealth tax dynamics — contested valuation, structured avoidance, political resistance — despite the superior architecture. The WDT without the labour dividend is not a stable long-run equilibrium; it is a transitional state moving toward conventional wealth tax failure.

#### §6.4 China as Empirical Illustration: The Trajectory Confirms the Theory

China's trajectory from the Deng era through to the present is the most instructive available case, and its recent direction confirms rather than complicates the INST argument.

During the Deng-through-Hu period (approximately 1978–2012), China exhibited developmental authoritarian characteristics: deliberate separation of economic and political power, credible property rights for the private sector, and institutional constraints on arbitrary extraction sufficient to attract the private capital formation that drove growth. This is the period during which a WDT-compatible fiscal architecture was most conceivable — though the governance conditions for full implementation (mandatory public transparency, independent institutional oversight with real blocking power) were not present.

The Xi era has moved sharply in the opposite direction. Beginning with the 2021 "common prosperity" campaign and regulatory crackdowns on tech, property and education (wiping over $1 trillion in market capitalisation from China's largest private firms), extending through demands for "golden shares" and party committees within private companies, and culminating in the revised National Defence Mobilization Law of August 2026 — which allows the state to requisition or expropriate civilian resources including transport infrastructure, manufacturing capacity, data and technical personnel, and extends mobilisation triggers to threats to "development interests" as well as sovereignty — China has progressively destroyed the credible commitment infrastructure that distinguished developmental authoritarianism from predatory extraction.

The mobilisation law is analytically decisive for INST purposes because it makes the incompatibility of the WDT's Condition 3 with China's current governance architecture explicit and legal rather than merely probable. A private wealth holder under this framework cannot treat any fiscal commitment — including a WDT-style refund guarantee — as protected against state requisition, because the same legal framework that would guarantee the refund explicitly reserves the state's right to compel capital redeployment. The two commitments are structurally incompatible: the WDT's symmetric refund guarantee requires the state to commit to returning a proportional share of wealth losses; the mobilisation law reserves the state's right to expropriate those same assets for state purposes with compensation at the state's discretion.

The capital flight response confirms the mechanism: estimated illicit outflows of $254 billion in the four quarters to June 2024 (Catalyst International, 2025), declining private fixed investment, and the JPMorgan classification of major Chinese internet stocks as "uninvestable" in 2022 — all consistent with a wealthy population responding to credible commitment failure by restructuring and exiting. This is exactly the avoidance behaviour the WDT's cooperative architecture was designed to prevent, and its emergence is the predicted consequence of destroying the credible commitment preconditions.

China has therefore crossed back from Tier 3 into Tier 4 in terms of WDT compatibility, and has done so by precisely the mechanism the INST argument predicts: an authoritarian state recognising that sophisticated fiscal architecture generates accountability pressure responded by closing the institutional space rather than accepting the pressure. The mobilisation law is pre-emptive suppression of WDT-compatible conditions — the domestic institutional analogue of FM's Option B at the interstate competitive level.

### §7 The Three Competitive Advantages

**Purpose:** State what democratic systems with WDT would gain, as precisely as the available evidence supports. Three distinct advantages, all conditional on Phase One demonstration. The welfare endurance advantage is new to this section and addresses the authoritarian cost-imposition capacity directly.

**Content:**

**Advantage 1: The resource pool.**
RATES establishes four properties simultaneously at the 2000 worst-case reference: SRR fills at year 3 invariantly across all 73 historical start years; 100% success rate (LRR fills and never reaches zero) across all start years; post-fill surplus 6–15% of government expenditure at the hardest start, 125.5% at the median; revenue-weighted annual wealth burden 0.35% of net worth, gain-weighted effective lifetime rate 13.0% — both below comparable alternatives on a materially larger base (RATES §10).

The order-of-magnitude resource claim: the pre-behavioural combined estimate (individual WDT ~£874b, corporate levy ~£172b, UHNW tail ~£27b) approaches near-parity with UK total managed expenditure of ~£1,157b (RATES §8.3). A democratic state with WDT access to this pool does not need to match authoritarian concentration ratios to achieve competitive resource parity. It needs sufficient absolute resources that the concentration advantage becomes less decisive than the total deployable pool.

**Advantage 2: The welfare floor — eliminating the endurance asymmetry.**
Authoritarian systems' demonstrated capacity to run populations at lower welfare thresholds has historically been a genuine competitive advantage, particularly in crisis scenarios. Democratic systems must maintain majority welfare even under fiscal stress. This constraint has been funded through three mechanisms — labour and consumption taxation, borrowing, and inflation — each of which imposes costs on the majority that erode welfare directly or through time.

The WDT eliminates this constraint at the structural level. The labour tax relief dividend (ENV §4) actively increases majority welfare simultaneously with increasing state fiscal capacity. The displacement channel is metered through constitutionally committed LRR accumulation (ENV §3.1), delivering incremental welfare improvements rather than abrupt shock. There is no prior fiscal architecture that moves fiscal capacity and majority welfare in the same direction simultaneously.

The "not paid for via inflation" dimension is load-bearing: conventional democratic crisis responses — QE, emergency borrowing — impose majority welfare costs through inflation and future fiscal consolidation. A WDT-funded crisis response draws on the wealth delta of the top percentiles rather than majority purchasing power. The majority's welfare position is not the adjustment variable. The authoritarian endurance advantage is specifically an advantage in scenarios where the governing system can impose costs on the majority without electoral consequence. The WDT narrows this advantage by reducing the probability that resource mobilisation requires majority welfare sacrifice.

**Advantage 3: Pre-built crisis capacity.**
The SRR and LRR architecture (RATES §6) provides pre-accumulated fiscal reserves that compound over the system's operating life. A democracy that has operated WDT for a decade before a crisis enters with a qualitatively different mobilisation position than one starting from zero. This advantage is strongly path-dependent — it accrues only to systems that commit early and maintain the mechanism through accumulation (FM §4).

The Andersson & Teorell (2025) finding on state capacity is relevant here: stronger state capacity prolongs individual autocratic rule but can increase the probability of eventual democratisation. The WDT-equivalent for democratic systems is that accumulated SRR/LRR capacity makes democratic systems more resilient in the crisis scenarios where authoritarian flexibility was previously decisive, while the accountability mechanisms sustain the cooperative compliance that keeps the reserves funded.

**Tier A:** RATES §2, §8.3, §10; ENV §3, §4; FM §4. Andersson & Teorell (2025).
**Tier B:** The competitive framing and endurance asymmetry analysis.

### §8 Memetic Diffusion and the Race Condition

**Purpose:** What happens when the structural argument is recognised by governments operating under competitive pressure? Four response options; their dynamics and costs.

**Content:**

FM establishes a binary for any jurisdiction concluding the WDT's properties are likely true: commit or suppress, with no stable middle (FM §1). The present paper extends that binary to the systemic level. When multiple governance systems simultaneously recognise that one system type has acquired or is acquiring a durable structural advantage, four response options become available:

**Option A — Adopt.** Implement the full WDT architecture — fiscal, governance, and dividend — accepting the democratic constraints that requires. The FM non-decomposability result applies: partial adoption of the fiscal architecture without the governance architecture produces an unstable hybrid approaching conventional wealth tax failure modes (FM §2). The labour dividend cannot be omitted without losing the majority constituency that makes the mechanism politically durable.

**Option B — Suppress.** Prevent recognition of the advantage through information control, counter-narrative, and isolation from jurisdictions demonstrating it. Viable only until the advantage becomes observable in outcomes rather than arguable from architecture. The suppression strategy has a structural weakness (FM §3.2): it is operated by a small professional class with concentrated policy access; the constituency that would eventually defend the WDT mechanism is numerically large and electorally decisive once welfare consequences are visible. China's mobilisation law revision is interpretable as domestic Option B — pre-emptive suppression of the institutional conditions under which WDT adoption would become conceivable.

**Option C — Race.** Attempt WDT-compatible implementation rapidly enough to capture the advantage before early adopters accumulate insurmountable reserve differentials. Non-symmetric: the lifetime contribution envelope deepens with every year of system membership; the cooperative compliance norm strengthens with every assessed cycle; the membrane investment (BEHAV §11.1) cannot be deferred and pays returns that a late entrant has not yet made. Kato & Tanaka's caveat applies: taxation doesn't mechanically cause democratic transition, and Option C requires accepting governance constraints that are not merely technical but institutional. A Tier 3 state attempting Option C begins the trajectory the INST argument predicts.

**Option D — Conflict.** Attempt to deny the advantage to existing adopters through active disruption before the reserve differential becomes deterrent. Historically available; the Andersson & Teorell (2025) double-edged-sword result is relevant — stronger WDT-generated state capacity in democratic systems may initially appear threatening to authoritarian competitors before the accountability dynamic becomes visible. The deterrent against Option D is the accumulated reserve differential itself, which is why the bootstrapping vulnerability window (POL §6) is the paper's most consequential near-term risk.

The diffusion pattern under competitive pressure is likely non-linear: fastest adoption at the Tier 2/3 boundary, where institutional proximity to WDT requirements is greatest; slowest at the Tier 4/5 core, where Option B is institutionally feasible and adoption is structurally self-defeating. The advantage concentrates in systems already closest to Tier 1, deepening the differential rather than closing it.

The paper's final hypothesis: the WDT may be the missing tool that democracies have not yet realised they are missing. Its significance, if correct, is not primarily fiscal. It is that changing the fiscal architecture changes the incentives that determine which political institutions can sustain themselves over time. Whether that change is large enough to alter the long-run balance between governance system types cannot be established from desk research. It requires Phase One implementation, demonstrated track record, and the comparative evidence on economic performance, political institutions, and state capacity that only a live system can generate.

**Tier B:** Options A–D structural dynamics.
**Tier C:** Diffusion pattern, path-dependence claim, final hypothesis.
**WDT references:** FM §1–§4; POL §6; BEHAV §11.1; RATES §6; GOV §3.2.
**External literature:** Kato & Tanaka (2019); Andersson & Teorell (2025).

### §9 Failure Conditions

**Purpose:** Name what would falsify the thesis. A genuine engagement with the conditions under which the paper is wrong, not a pro-forma caveat section.

**Content:** Twelve falsifying conditions in three categories.

**Mechanism failures:**
1. Phase One generates no cooperative compliance shift — tolerant zone and refund-protection asymmetry do not produce predicted declaration behaviour (PHASE1 §4.1; OQ #4, #17).
2. Valuation capture — Route D auction fails credibility; Routes A/B systematically gamed (VAL §14.4; OQ #13, #23).
3. SRR cannot sustain stress beyond 1947–2019 UK historical range (RATES §9.4, §7.2 qualification).
4. Membrane failure from first contact — cooperative norm never established during Phase One (BEHAV §10.1, §11.1; PHASE1 §4.5).

**Competitive mechanism failures:**
5. Labour tax welfare effects do not materialise — ENV displacement channel does not produce predicted output at scale (ENV §4; PHASE1 implicit).
6. Higher private wealth does not reduce welfare-state demand — feedback loop from private resilience to reduced required government expenditure does not operate at meaningful magnitude. *No Phase One measurement design currently addresses this directly — assigned to MACRO.*
7. Authoritarian adaptation — authoritarian systems develop alternative mechanisms for accessing the upper-tail wealth pool that do not require WDT-compatible institutions. This failure condition is the one where the structural exclusion argument is most vulnerable to future developments not currently modelled.
8. Cross-base migration externality too large — Agrawal et al. (2025) six-to-one multiplier applies to WDT population at a magnitude that erodes the revenue base before the reserve differential accumulates (BEHAV §9.2; OQ #5; PHASE1 §4.3, §5.3). The Jakobsen et al. (forthcoming AER) finding of ~2% reduction in wealthy taxpayer stock per 1pp rate increase provides the empirical ballpark; the WDT-specific figure is a Phase One unknown.

**Evolutionary mechanism failures:**
9. Democratic WDT systems do not demonstrate materially superior outcomes on timescales that drive institutional imitation (FM §4; INST Cluster 5).
10. Option D chosen before reserve differential becomes deterrent — vulnerability window exploited before it closes (POL §6.7; FM §1).
11. Bootstrapping problem unsolved — no jurisdiction commits to Phase One during sufficient political stability (POL §6.3; FM §1 binary).
12. Capital pool premise wrong — pool is not as large or accessible as mechanism papers establish (RATES §3.1, §5.1; JUR §5).

### §10 The Research Programme

**Purpose:** Convert the hypothesis into testable propositions. The paper's contribution is a well-specified research programme, not a proof.

**Five empirical clusters:**

**Cluster 1 — Mechanism performance.** Does Phase One generate predicted cooperative compliance? Observable: declaration ratio distribution (α) versus SWEEPS predictions; refund uptake in loss years; route adoption distribution. Primary Phase One deliverable (PHASE1 §5.1, §5.2; OQ #4, #13, #18, #29).

**Cluster 2 — Welfare effects.** Does the labour dividend produce measurable majority welfare improvement and reduction in welfare-state demand? Observable: disposable income by decile; private wealth accumulation below threshold; welfare-state demand indicators. Phase One and Phase Two deliverables (ENV §4, §5; OQ #11, #12 assigned to MACRO). Failure condition 6 gap must be addressed in MACRO design.

**Cluster 3 — Fiscal capacity differential.** Does a WDT-equipped democracy accumulate a reserve differential large enough to be competitively significant? Observable: SRR/LRR trajectories versus RATES predictions; cross-base externality magnitude (PHASE1 §5.3; OQ #5); effective mobilisation capacity in first stress scenario (RATES §7.3, §10).

**Cluster 4 — Governance compatibility under authoritarian pressure.** Do Tier 3 states attempting WDT implementation face the predicted endogenous institutional pressure toward democratic accountability? Do they respond with Option A, B, C, or D? Observable: institutional reform trajectories in early-adopter jurisdictions; cases where fiscal architecture is attempted without governance architecture. Comparative political economy; requires case study depth beyond WDT project scope (PHASE1 §6; OQ #31 — new, created by INST).

**Cluster 5 — Diffusion dynamics.** Does the predicted adoption pattern — fastest at Tier 2/3 boundary, slowest at Tier 4/5 core — emerge under competitive pressure? Observable: adoption sequencing across governance tiers; counter-narrative investment by non-adopting states; Option B deployment by Tier 4/5 governments. Long-run observable; untestable before Phase One produces demonstrable outcomes (FM §4; PHASE1 §2; OQ #32 — new, created by INST).

## Notable Gaps Remaining

Three claims lack complete support and require explicit Tier C labelling or future work:

1. **Failure condition 6** — welfare-state demand reduction feedback loop. No current ENV or PHASE1 measurement design addresses this. Assign explicitly to MACRO design requirement.

2. **Failure condition 7** — authoritarian adaptation to access the upper-tail wealth pool without WDT-compatible institutions. The paper must be explicit that this is the structural exclusion argument's most vulnerable point. The China mobilisation law trajectory is evidence in the opposite direction but is one case, not a systematic finding.

3. **The "not knowing a better alternative" equilibrium** (§2) — information suppression dynamics bearing on the sustainability of the authoritarian welfare endurance advantage as WDT consequences become visible. Needs either external communication economics / authoritarian information control literature or explicit Tier C labelling.
