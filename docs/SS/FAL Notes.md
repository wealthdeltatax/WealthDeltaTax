Yes. I think this should be a deliberately different paper from the others. It should **not defend WDT**. It should define the claims that would most seriously threaten it and hand them to hostile researchers as modelling targets.

# Proposed paper: **Falsifiable Hypotheses**

### Purpose

The paper establishes four major hypotheses on which the broader WDT proposal depends, identifies the mechanisms behind each, and defines what evidence or modelling would count against them.

The central methodological commitment should be:

> **WDT should be exposed to attempts to falsify its strongest claims rather than evaluated only through models constructed by its proponents.**

The paper should explicitly say that the author does not currently possess the resources to model all four questions adequately and that this is a feature of the research programme, not something to conceal.

---

# 1. Central research question

Something like:

> **Under what conditions would the behavioural, macroeconomic, institutional and welfare mechanisms proposed by Wealth Delta Tax fail?**

Then the paper asks four specific questions:

1. Would wealthy taxpayers prefer WDT to existing tax arrangements once all relevant costs are included?
2. Would WDT improve rather than damage macroeconomic efficiency and long-run economic performance?
3. Could WDT generate a stable cooperative equilibrium during and after its bootstrapping phase?
4. Does taxation of changes in wealth produce lower economic friction and higher welfare than taxation of income, realised gains, stocks of wealth or consumption?

The fourth needs slightly careful wording because "WDT is more efficient" is too broad. The actual hypothesis can be narrower and therefore more falsifiable.

---

# 2. Hypothesis I: Wealthy taxpayer preference

## Hypothesis

> **H1: Under plausible parameterisations, wealthy taxpayers will prefer WDT to the existing tax system when their total economic costs are considered rather than tax liability alone.**

The key insight is that the comparison should not be:

**WDT tax bill vs current tax bill.**

It should be:

**WDT total cost vs current system total cost.**

Include:

* taxes actually paid
* avoidance expenditure
* accounting costs
* legal costs
* compliance costs
* portfolio distortion
* realisation lock-in
* tax uncertainty
* expected future tax changes
* migration costs
* expected WDT refunds
* value of institutional participation
* potentially expected macroeconomic effects.

### Your specific conjecture

The current reference WDT model produces an approximately **0.35% annual wealth-equivalent burden** under its stated assumptions.

Your hypothesis is that existing wealthy taxpayers already incur an economically meaningful cost at least comparable to this through the combination of taxation and tax-induced friction.

### What would falsify it?

A hostile model should try to demonstrate that:

* WDT's total cost is systematically higher than the existing system;
* wealthy taxpayers have a strong economic incentive to leave;
* avoidance opportunities under WDT are sufficiently large to undermine the system;
* institutional participation has negligible value;
* WDT reduces expected lifetime wealth enough to make migration rational.

### Particularly valuable result

Find the **break-even WDT burden**.

For example:

> At what annual WDT burden does the representative £10m, £100m, £1bn and £10bn taxpayer become indifferent between WDT and the existing system?

That gives you a much better object than simply saying "wealthy people will like WDT."

---

# 3. Hypothesis II: Macroeconomic effects

## Hypothesis

> **H2: WDT's reduction of taxation-induced lock-in and other allocation frictions, combined with symmetric treatment of wealth gains and losses and subsequent labour-tax relief, produces a non-negative or positive long-run welfare and productivity effect relative to plausible alternative tax systems.**

This is probably the most ambitious hypothesis.

Your proposed causal chain is:

**WDT**

→ reduced realisation distortion
→ reduced lock-in
→ improved capital allocation
→ reduced tax-planning friction
→ increased productive investment / consumption
→ increased productivity
→ increased private returns
→ larger future WDT base
→ greater fiscal capacity
→ lower labour taxation / welfare expenditure
→ broader welfare gains.

But the paper should explicitly include the **opposing chain**:

**WDT**

→ reduced after-tax return
→ reduced investment
→ capital relocation
→ lower capital formation
→ lower productivity
→ lower wealth growth
→ smaller tax base.

The modeller's job is to determine which dominates.

### What would falsify it?

For plausible parameter ranges, demonstrate that WDT:

* materially reduces investment;
* produces persistent capital flight;
* reduces productivity;
* increases macroeconomic volatility;
* creates fiscal instability through refunds;
* produces welfare losses greater than the distortions it removes;
* or fails to generate the proposed positive feedback.

### Important distinction

Don't claim:

> WDT stabilises the economy.

Claim:

> **WDT is hypothesised to possess stabilising transmission mechanisms that require quantitative evaluation.**

That leaves the paper genuinely falsifiable.

---

# 4. Hypothesis III: Cooperative institutional equilibrium

This is the really unusual one.

## Hypothesis

> **H3: A WDT jurisdiction can reach and maintain a stable cooperative equilibrium in which wealthy taxpayers have sufficient long-term incentives to preserve the credibility of the WDT/SWF institution rather than collectively attempting to undermine, capture or exit it.**

This is where your sovereign-voice argument belongs.

The taxpayer potentially receives:

* institutional representation;
* a claim on the SWF;
* symmetric downside protection;
* a more predictable fiscal regime;
* lower tax-induced economic friction.

The hypothesis is therefore **not**:

> Rich people will behave altruistically.

It is:

> **The repeated-game value of preserving the institution can exceed the short-term gain from individually exploiting or capturing it.**

That is much more interesting.

### Model

A serious model should allow:

* individual capture;
* collective capture;
* lobbying;
* corruption;
* strategic exit;
* strategic compliance;
* reputation;
* taxpayer coordination;
* institutional credibility;
* SWF returns;
* future tax liabilities;
* future refund claims;
* political intervention.

Then ask:

> Does a stable cooperative equilibrium exist?

And under what conditions does it disappear?

### Bootstrapping

This should be a major subsection.

The model should explicitly examine:

**Phase One → credible institution → taxpayer cooperation**

versus

**Phase One → low initial credibility → taxpayer exit/avoidance → weak SWF → lower credibility → institutional failure.**

The hypothesis is that the first equilibrium is attainable under plausible initial conditions.

### What would falsify it?

Show that:

* individual capture is always privately optimal;
* cooperative behaviour cannot be sustained;
* wealthy taxpayers have incentives to exit before institutional benefits materialise;
* the SWF cannot become credible quickly enough;
* or a small group can profitably destroy institutional credibility.

That would be a very serious result against WDT.

---

# 5. Hypothesis IV: Accumulation-point efficiency

I'd make this one the most theoretically disciplined.

## Hypothesis

> **H4: Conditional on a comparable comprehensive economic base and appropriately specified redistribution, taxing changes in wealth produces lower aggregate distortion than taxing intermediate manifestations of the same economic process, particularly realised gains and existing wealth stocks.**

The mechanism is:

**economic accumulation**

↓

**wealth increase**

↓

**WDT tax**

rather than:

**accumulation**

↓

**asset holding**

↓

**realisation / transaction / income event**

↓

**tax**

The hypothesis is that moving the tax point closer to the underlying change in economic capacity reduces the number of behavioural decisions that become tax-relevant.

This is where your "friction / entropy" intuition can be translated into something economists can actually test.

### What the modeller should measure

For equivalent revenue, compare:

* asset allocation distortion;
* realisation distortion;
* lock-in;
* administrative complexity;
* avoidance expenditure;
* transaction costs;
* compliance costs;
* deadweight loss;
* welfare;
* capital allocation;
* tax-base elasticity.

### What would falsify it?

A model showing that, after accounting for:

* valuation,
* liquidity,
* risk,
* migration,
* avoidance,
* administrative costs,
* behavioural responses,

WDT generates **greater total distortion** than the alternative systems over relevant parameter ranges.

That is a clean falsification criterion.

That is important because it changes the relationship between your work and outside researchers.

# 8. The paper should also define what would count as a serious result

This is important because otherwise you'll be accused of moving the goalposts.

I'd establish three possible outcomes.

### Support

The hostile model finds the hypothesis holds across a substantial range of empirically defensible parameters.

### Conditional support

The hypothesis holds only under identifiable conditions, such as:

* migration elasticity below X;
* compliance cost below Y;
* WDT burden below Z;
* SWF reserve above Q.

### Falsification

The hypothesis fails across empirically defensible parameter ranges, or requires assumptions that are themselves implausible.

And critically:

**"The model can be made to work" should not count as support.**

That's too weak.

Not:

Something much more direct:

That last sentence feels like the correct intellectual posture for this stage of the project.

## 1. "The rich will leave" may actually be the wrong null hypothesis

Your hypothesis is approximately:

> A wealthy taxpayer compares the *total economic cost* of WDT with the total economic cost of the existing tax environment.

That is much more interesting than simply asking whether WDT causes migration.

Suppose WDT imposes an expected annual burden equivalent to 0.35% of net worth.

Now suppose the existing system imposes:

* tax planning,
* accountants,
* lawyers,
* trusts and holding companies,
* realisation timing,
* portfolio distortions,
* CGT lock-in,
* inheritance planning,
* residency planning,
* compliance costs,
* uncertainty about future tax rules.

Then **0.35% is not necessarily a tax increase in the economically relevant sense.**

It could be a reduction in the *total cost of being wealthy*.

And your Project Map already gives the 0.35% figure as the revenue-weighted annual burden in the reference scenario. ([Wealth Delta Tax (WDT)][1])

The crucial research question becomes:

> **What is the existing all-in effective friction cost of wealth ownership for a very wealthy UK taxpayer?**

I would absolutely want somebody to model that.

Because if the answer is, say, 0.7%, your migration hypothesis suddenly becomes quite interesting.

If it is 0.1%, your argument gets much harder.

And importantly, **you don't need to establish the answer yourself.**

You can effectively say:

> Here is my hypothesis. Here is the threshold at which it becomes true. Someone please calculate it.

That's excellent research positioning.

# 2. The sovereign voice hypothesis is actually more interesting than "the rich will like WDT"

I think you have identified something potentially quite powerful here.

The WDT taxpayer isn't simply paying a tax.

They are acquiring a continuing relationship with an institution whose capital partly comes from their own contributions.

Your Project Map explicitly makes taxpayer participation in the SWF part of the institutional architecture. ([Wealth Delta Tax (WDT)][1])

Your hypothesis is basically:

> **A wealthy person may rationally prefer partial institutional voice over having no institutional claim whatsoever, particularly when they expect to remain wealthy for decades.**

And then there is the really interesting second-order effect:

> They may initially try to capture the institution because capture is individually attractive, but eventually discover that the value of the institution depends upon its credibility.

That creates a potential **repeated-game equilibrium**.

Something like:

**Individual incentive:**
"Can I manipulate this?"

↓

**Collective wealthy-class incentive:**
"Wait, if everyone destroys credibility, the institution becomes less valuable."

↓

**Long-term incentive:**
"Protect the institution because I own a large contingent claim on its future functioning."

That's exactly the sort of thing game theorists could model.

And they could try very hard to break it.

For example:

> What happens if one wealthy taxpayer can gain £X through capture while imposing only £Y on everyone else?

If X > Y, your cooperative equilibrium might collapse.

But if institutional credibility is itself an asset whose value rises with the number of wealthy participants, you may get the opposite result.

**That is a fascinating research problem.**

# 3. Your macro hypothesis is probably the most ambitious

Here I would be much more cautious than you are.

Your proposed chain is:

**WDT**

→ less lock-in
→ less tax-planning friction
→ more efficient capital allocation
→ better investment allocation
→ more consumption
→ greater productivity
→ greater profits
→ greater wealth growth
→ larger WDT base
→ more revenue
→ lower labour taxation
→ higher middle/lower income
→ lower welfare expenditure
→ greater fiscal capacity

That's an enormous positive feedback loop.

And importantly, your ENV paper already acknowledges that it is identifying transmission channels rather than estimating their magnitudes, and explicitly says it is not a general-equilibrium model. ([Wealth Delta Tax (WDT)][2])

That is exactly where I would want another research group to attack.

Not:

> "Can you prove this?"

But:

> **"Can you construct a general-equilibrium model in which these mechanisms are all allowed to operate simultaneously, and see whether the positive feedback survives?"**

Because there are obvious ways it could fail.

For example:

### Your positive loop

Lower distortion
→ higher productivity
→ higher wealth returns.

But:

### Possible negative loop

WDT
→ lower after-tax expected return
→ lower capital formation
→ lower investment
→ lower productivity
→ lower wealth growth
→ smaller WDT base.

The fascinating question is **which equilibrium dominates**.

And I don't think your current project should pretend to know.

You should actively invite someone to try to kill the positive result.

# 4. The macro question is especially attractive because WDT has a strange automatic stabiliser

This part of your hypothesis deserves serious attention.

A conventional income tax:

**boom → higher income → higher tax**

but also:

**recession → lower income → lower tax**

WDT potentially has:

**asset boom → higher wealth delta → higher tax**

and:

**asset crash → negative wealth delta → refund.**

That means the fiscal system is mechanically linked to asset-market conditions.

Your refund mechanism therefore potentially creates a **state contingent fiscal position** rather than merely a progressive tax.

Your Project Map explicitly treats the symmetric refund as a core structural feature, rather than an optional insurance mechanism. ([Wealth Delta Tax (WDT)][1])

But again, this desperately needs modelling.

Because there are competing effects.

During a crash:

**WDT refunds → household liquidity → stabilisation**

but simultaneously:

**wealthy households receive large fiscal transfers → perhaps stabilising demand**

while:

**government fiscal position deteriorates → perhaps contractionary elsewhere**

unless the SWF absorbs the shock.

And if the SWF has accumulated reserves during good years, you've potentially constructed something resembling a **private-wealth-linked sovereign automatic stabiliser**.

That is much more interesting than simply "a wealth tax."

Someone should model it.

# 5. And this changes how I see your "bootstrapping" problem

You hypothesise:

> wealthy taxpayers will actually help fund the system through Phase One because they expect to benefit from it later.

I initially treated that as a potentially circular assumption.

But your argument is stronger if you frame it as an **investment decision**.

Imagine a wealthy taxpayer has:

* £100m wealth
* WDT cost of 0.35% = £350k/year
* existing avoidance/compliance/lock-in costs
* substantial exposure to future market losses
* a multi-decade horizon.

They aren't deciding:

> "Do I like paying £350k?"

They're deciding:

> **"What institutional arrangement maximises the expected value of my wealth over the next 30 years?"**

That is a completely different problem.

And then the SWF becomes potentially important.

If they believe:

**I contribute £350k/year**

but receive:

**lower distortion + lower labour taxation in the economy + more stable fiscal conditions + downside participation + institutional voice + a more predictable tax regime**

then they could rationally support the institution.

Not because they're altruistic.

Because they're **buying an equilibrium**.

That is potentially a very strong argument.

But again, it needs a game-theoretic model.

# 6. Your fourth hypothesis needs one important modification

You said:

> "undoubtably, because its mathematical and entropic driven rational. taxing anywhere but the accumulation is less efficient"

This is the one I would **not** let you leave in that form.

Not because the intuition is necessarily wrong.

Because "undoubtedly" is doing too much.

The strongest defensible version is something like:

> **For a given comprehensive economic income base, taxation closer to the point of economic accumulation may reduce certain behavioural and administrative distortions associated with taxing intermediate manifestations of that accumulation.**

That's a proposition someone can actually investigate.

Your lock-in argument is particularly strong because it has a concrete mechanism.

Realisation taxation creates:

**tax event → disposal decision → distorted asset allocation.**

Accrual taxation breaks that connection.

Your WFR modelling already finds substantial lock-in welfare costs under CGT in its controlled model. The Project Map reports roughly 141 to 143 bp for the scenarios it examined. ([Wealth Delta Tax (WDT)][1])

That's a much better argument than "entropy says WDT is optimal."

Because the former gives someone something they can attempt to falsify.

I think you have accidentally arrived at a **research programme other people could actually pick up.**

You don't need to solve all of this.

You can basically say:

### Hypothesis A: Wealthy taxpayer equilibrium

Compare:

**Current system total cost**

vs.

**WDT total cost**

including tax, compliance, avoidance, lock-in, legal costs, uncertainty, migration and expected refund value.

Then determine the wealth level at which WDT becomes privately preferable.

### Hypothesis B: Wealth migration equilibrium

Construct a model in which wealthy taxpayers choose:

**stay + WDT**

versus

**leave + foreign tax system**

with endogenous effects on:

* wealth returns
* professional ecosystem
* investment
* labour
* consumption
* fiscal revenue.

Then ask whether WDT can have **positive net attraction** under plausible parameters.

There is already evidence that wealth-tax mobility is real, so this isn't something you can simply assume away. Agrawal, Foremny and Martínez-Toledano find substantial mobility responses and a much larger cross-base revenue effect in the Spanish regional wealth-tax setting. ([TopCat][3])

But their result is not a direct WDT result.

**That's precisely why somebody should model WDT separately.**

Build a DSGE / heterogeneous-agent / overlapping-generations model containing:

* WDT

* SWF
* labour-tax replacement
* capital allocation
* heterogeneous returns
* investment
* consumption
* migration
* government expenditure.

Then ask:

> Does WDT produce a higher long-run welfare equilibrium?

That would be *extremely* valuable.

# And this is where your project becomes much more interesting to me

You don't actually need to say:

> "I have proven WDT works."

You can say something considerably more intellectually respectable:

> **"Here is a mechanism which appears theoretically coherent. Here are the empirical uncertainties. Here are the specific models required to determine whether the mechanism survives them."**

Your site already does a lot of this. The Project Map explicitly labels six remaining literature gaps rather than pretending they have been solved. ([Wealth Delta Tax (WDT)][1])

And your BEHAV paper makes an especially important methodological move: it defines behavioural robustness as functioning across different behavioural responses rather than depending on one assumed response. ([Wealth Delta Tax (WDT)][4])

So the really good next stage isn't **you trying to model everything**.

It's:

> **Make WDT sufficiently explicit that somebody who hates it can build the model that kills it.**

Because if a hostile economist takes your assumptions, gives themselves every opportunity to introduce:

* migration,
* avoidance,
* capital flight,
* lower investment,
* political capture,
* strategic valuation,
* fiscal instability,

and the system **still comes out viable**, then you've obtained something much more valuable than another ten papers defending WDT.

You've obtained an adversarial result.

And if they kill it, that's valuable too.

### The strongest possible outcome is not "WDT survives your own modelling."

It's:

**"Here is the strongest model we could construct against WDT. We gave it every reasonable opportunity to fail. Under these parameter ranges it still survives. Here are the ranges where it doesn't."**

That is the kind of result that makes economists pay attention.

And honestly, given that your own Project Map now says **all currently planned papers are complete and the remaining gaps are specifically identified**, this is probably the natural point for the project to stop trying to close every gap itself and start making the gaps *legible to people capable of closing them*.
