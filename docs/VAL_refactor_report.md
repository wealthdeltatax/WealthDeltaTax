## Findings for the paper
1. **Net tax (C.1) is a poor incentive metric for understaters.** At N = 30, α = 0.8 shows a net-tax *saving* at g = 8–14% (−0.01 to −0.15%) and α = 0.5 at g = 10–11%, while TW says −2.4% / −6.1% and corrected PV says +0.6% / +1.5%. Net tax counts credited £, which understates what an understater really surrenders.
2. **Corrected C.12 has the same sign as the TW gap in 74 of 76 cells** (legacy: 21 of 76). It is smooth and monotone: understaters pay more in PV, overstaters pay less, at every g ≥ 0.4%.
3. **Consequence: the "timing artefact" claim is not supported.** In this model mild overstatement has a real advantage (TW +2.4% at α = 1.2, +5.9% at α = 1.5, +12% at α = 2.0 at g = 10.4%; PV −0.57% / −1.42% / −2.78%). It survives discounting.
4. **Likely mechanism (needs your judgement).** Under Route C the fraction of the asset surrendered each year is q = L/W, which is almost independent of α (f_N(α)/f_N(1) ≈ 0.99), but the credited liability scales with α. The lifetime refund cap and the sell-year refund are computed on the credited total, so an overstater is refunded on more £ than the equity they actually surrendered. Whether refunds/caps should be based on true-value contributions is a design question, not a code bug. It bears on VAL §5.2, §7.3, VAL.A §A.2.4–A.2.5, §A.5.4, §A.6 and the D.3 mechanism case ("overstater transfers more equity").
5. **The two metrics genuinely disagree only at g ≥ 25%, α ≤ 0.2** (understater retains *more* TW than honest while paying more net tax and more PV). This is the rate-ceiling region, well outside the policy range.
6. **Prose claims that now match the tables:** C.1 crossing for α = 1.2/1.5 at g ≈ 7%; C.11.4 = C.8 at N = 30 exactly; C.10.1 honest TW = C.10.2 N = 30 row (89.36).
7. **Prose to re-check:** N-crossing values (N ≈ 19.5/20/20.8, from SWEEPS.A), plateau inflection (17.3% vs 19.1%), C.9 plateau ceilings (98% vs 38% in Fig 7.2b), all "timing artefact" language.

## Addendum: discount-rate sweep and state tolerance zone (revenue as the governing metric)

Rationale: the state's interest is present-value revenue relative to what an honest declarer would pay. C.12 (PV of true value surrendered) is therefore the headline metric; net tax and terminal wealth are context.

Added: `rho_sweep = [0, 2, 3, 5, 7, 10]%` and `tol_pp = 2` in `WDT_Params.toml`; `run_pair_rhos` in `wdt_core.py` (one simulation, PV at every rho); `tables['c12_rho']` and `charts['fig_7_4_tolerance_zone']` in `val_core.py`; Table C.12b, Fig 7.4 and Fig 7.5 in the generators. Two new validation checks (rho sweep at 5% equals C.12; honest row zero at every rho): 19 checks pass; all earlier tables and charts are byte-identical.

Results (N = 30, V0 = £20m, k = 0.001):
- Overstatement cells (alpha in (1, 2], g <= 25%) costing the state more than 2pp of TW(1): 88% at rho = 0, 77% at 2%, 69% at 3%, 41% at 5%, 9% at 7%, 0% at 10%. Worst shortfall -32.3pp, -17.5, -13.0, -7.1, -3.9, -1.6.
- At hist. mean g (10.45%), alpha = 2.0: state PV -13.0pp (rho 0), -6.97 (2%), -2.78 (5%), -0.61 (10%). Understaters always pay the state more PV.
- Declarer's TW-maximising alpha* is at the grid edge: 2.0 for g up to about 26%, 0.1 above. So the true optimum is outside [0.1, 2.0]. In the g >= 27% / low-alpha regime the two metrics disagree in sign (declarer retains more TW and the state receives more PV); treat with caution, as before.
- The revenue leak from overstatement is governed by rho relative to g: the state is indifferent to timing only when rho is near the growth rate.

### Addendum 3: declaration under uncertainty (market reveals true value)

Motivation: the constant-alpha model implicitly lets the taxpayer know V_t. In practice the taxpayer sets the declaration and the market sets the realised ratio alpha_t = D_t / V_t.

Added: `declared_path`, `simulate_declared`, `run_declared`, `npv_true_declared`, `run_declared_pair` in `wdt_core.py`; Monte Carlo `_compute_uncertainty` in `val_core.py` (lognormal returns, mean g = 10.45%, sigma in {0, 8.3% (historical std), 15%, 25%}, 1000 paired paths each, seed in the TOML); Table C.13 and Fig 7.7. Two rules: committed (D_t = m V0 (1+g)^t) and anchored (D_t = m V_{t-1} (1+g)). Check added: sigma = 0 reproduces the constant-alpha results to 4e-14 for both rules; 21 checks pass; all earlier outputs unchanged.

Results (N = 30, V0 = £20m, k = 0.001, rho = 5%, tolerance 2pp):
- Uncertainty does not bound overstatement. The declarer's best m is the grid maximum (5) on both mean and 5th-percentile TW gain, for every sigma and both rules.
- Uncertainty raises the margin needed for overstatement to work. Committed, P(TW gain): m = 1.18 is 100% at sigma 8.3%, 70% at 15%; m = 3 is needed for 99% at 25%. Anchored, m = 1.18 is about 100% at every sigma.
- Committed paths leak revenue even with no overstatement intent. At m = 1, P(state short > 2pp) is 3% (sigma 8.3%), 24% (15%), 51% (25%). Anchored at m = 1: 0%, 0%, 9%. Anchoring declarations to observed values removes most of this leak.
- Means at high sigma are dominated by the upper tail (committed, sigma 25%, m = 1: mean +27%, 5th percentile -10%). Read with the percentiles.

Assumptions to revisit: iid lognormal returns; observed value is known annually at no cost (anchored); honest declarer values exactly; no state true-up or clawback of refunds against revealed value (not modelled).
