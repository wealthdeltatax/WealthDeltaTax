# 1. The closest literature to your core argument

## Besley & Persson — state capacity is endogenous to political institutions

This is probably one of the most important foundations for INST.

Besley and Persson's *The Origins of State Capacity: Property Rights, Taxation, and Politics* develops a framework in which taxation and legal capacity are **not fixed characteristics of states**. They are investments shaped by political conditions. They find that inclusive political institutions, political stability and common-interest public goods are conducive to building fiscal capacity. ([Top Cat][1])

[Besley & Persson, “The Origins of State Capacity”](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.99.4.1218&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is extremely useful for INST because it supports the basic conceptual move:

**political institutions ↔ fiscal architecture ↔ state capacity**

rather than treating taxation as merely an instrument chosen *inside* an otherwise fixed political system.

### Relevance to your paper

**Strong support.**

Your argument goes one step further:

> If different fiscal instruments have different institutional requirements, fiscal architecture can itself alter the relative fitness of political systems.

Besley & Persson give you the theoretical foundation for taking that question seriously.

# 2. Even more directly relevant: taxation can create commitment problems for autocrats

## Per Andersson — *Fiscal capacity in non-democratic states*

This is probably the **single most important paper I found for INST**.

Andersson studies the introduction and expansion of income taxation in autocratic states. The key argument is that taxation creates a commitment problem: rulers have to convince elite supporters that new fiscal powers won't subsequently be used opportunistically against them.

The empirical analysis covers up to 25 autocracies from 1870–2012 and finds that stronger institutional oversight is associated with greater likelihood of introducing income taxation and greater tax revenue. ([Cambridge University Press][2])

[Andersson, “Fiscal capacity in non-democratic states”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-institutional-economics/article/fiscal-capacity-in-nondemocratic-states-the-origins-and-expansion-of-the-income-tax/0DEF81068289F1DFD9104A500A745687?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is almost eerily close to your Condition 3.

His argument is essentially:

> taxation requires credible constraints on the ruler's ability to abuse the fiscal instrument.

Your argument is:

> WDT requires credible constraints preventing the government from withholding refunds or otherwise altering the cooperative bargain.

The difference is important:

**Andersson:** authoritarian systems *can* solve the commitment problem, provided they develop sufficient institutional oversight.

**INST:** WDT may require a level/type of commitment that pushes the system toward democratic institutional constraints.

That means Andersson is both **supporting evidence and a challenge**.

You should absolutely cite him.

# 3. There is direct evidence that autocrats choose tax systems according to regime-survival incentives

## “Regime security and taxation in autocracies”

A 2021 *European Journal of Political Economy* paper examines government finances in **105 autocracies from 1950–2004**.

It finds that autocratic rulers choose tax structures partly according to whether elites or the mass population constitute the main threat to regime security. When elites are the threat, elite taxation is relaxed; when popular rebellion is the concern, taxation can be shifted toward elites. ([ScienceDirect][3])

[“Regime security and taxation in autocracies”](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0176268020301464?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is highly relevant to your Condition 4:

> **taxable population ≠ governing coalition**

The literature actually supports the underlying mechanism very nicely.

But there is an important qualification:

It demonstrates that authoritarian governments can **strategically manipulate taxation to preserve their coalition**.

Therefore you should not say:

> “Authoritarian governments cannot tax wealthy populations.”

The literature directly contradicts that.

The stronger claim is:

> **A fiscal system whose legitimacy and operation depend on the taxable population having enforceable institutional protection against the governing coalition may be structurally harder for authoritarian systems to sustain.**

That's much better.

# 4. China is probably your most important counterexample

There is a surprisingly substantial literature on exactly this issue.

## Bruce Gilley — *Taxation and Authoritarian Resilience*

Gilley examines China's tax system and argues that the CCP has maintained authoritarian resilience partly through a relatively non-intrusive tax structure that allows the government to provide services while limiting the political consequences of taxation.

He argues that moving toward more comprehensive taxation creates political difficulties because taxpayers begin demanding greater political representation. ([Taylor & Francis Online][4])

[Gilley, “Taxation and Authoritarian Resilience”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10670564.2016.1245891?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That is **extremely useful for INST**.

It suggests a mechanism very close to yours:

> **More sophisticated taxation → greater fiscal extraction → greater taxpayer-state interaction → pressure for representation/accountability.**

That doesn't prove WDT is uniquely democratic.

But it provides external literature for the broader proposition that **tax architecture can create political-institutional pressure.**

# 5. Changdong Zhang pushes this even further

Zhang's *A Fiscal Sociological Theory of Authoritarian Resilience* specifically treats taxation as a causal component of authoritarian durability.

He identifies two problems:

* a **representation dilemma**: stronger taxation can generate demands for political representation;
* a **growth dilemma**: taxation can undermine economic performance.

His proposed explanation for China's durability is essentially that the regime has constructed a partially developed / “half-tax” system that allows it to obtain fiscal resources while avoiding some of the political consequences of a comprehensive tax state. ([Sage Journals][5])

[Zhang, “A Fiscal Sociological Theory of Authoritarian Resilience”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0735275117693038?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is **very strong support for the conceptual architecture of INST**.

But again, it gives you a challenge:

> Authoritarian systems may not need to reproduce a democratic tax architecture. They may instead deliberately maintain a different fiscal architecture that preserves authoritarian resilience.

That's precisely the alternative hypothesis INST needs to confront.

# 6. There is also a newer literature saying state capacity can actually make autocracies stronger

This is important because it prevents you from making the simplistic argument:

> “Democracy produces state capacity; authoritarianism doesn't.”

That's demonstrably too strong.

A 2025 *Journal of Conflict Resolution* paper by Andersson & Teorell finds a **double-edged relationship**:

> stronger state capacity prolongs individual autocratic rule but can increase the probability of eventual democratization.

Their argument is that stronger states make rulers harder to overthrow by force, but also change elite incentives toward peaceful democratic transition. ([Sage Journals][6])

That is fascinating for your argument because it potentially gives INST a much more sophisticated evolutionary mechanism:

**WDT → greater state capacity**

could initially **strengthen authoritarian competitors**, rather than simply weakening them.

But if the associated institutional requirements also increase political accountability, the long-run effect could be different.

That is a considerably better research question than “WDT makes democracies stronger.”

Your claim needs to remain specifically about **WDT's particular institutional requirements**.

### Counterargument B: Authoritarian regimes can solve commitment problems

This is probably the biggest theoretical threat.

Andersson explicitly finds that authoritarian systems with institutional oversight can develop greater fiscal capacity. ([Cambridge University Press][2])

So your Condition 3 currently risks being too strong.

The question becomes:

> **Does WDT require democratic accountability, or merely credible commitment?**

If it is merely the latter, an authoritarian state might reproduce the necessary commitment through elite bargaining.

You need to answer this directly.

### Counterargument C: Authoritarian systems can deliberately choose less intrusive taxation

China is the obvious example. Gilley and Zhang essentially say:

> authoritarian durability can depend upon *not* building a fully intrusive tax state. ([Taylor & Francis Online][4])

This actually gives you an alternative equilibrium:

**Democracy:** comprehensive taxation + accountability.

**Authoritarianism:** selective/indirect taxation + limited fiscal extraction + political autonomy.

The question isn't whether the second can survive.

It clearly can.

The question is whether the first eventually generates a **large enough performance differential** to create competitive pressure.

That is your actual evolutionary question.

The literature remains contested. The Oxford Handbook review explicitly describes the “authoritarian advantage” versus “democratic dividend” debate as unresolved. ([OUP Academic][18])

For example, one study finds democracy has a comparative advantage in differentiated-product exports, while electoral autocracy can outperform closed autocracy in some circumstances. So I would **not** make “democracies are inherently competitively superior” a premise.

Your argument is much more interesting:

> **Perhaps a particular fiscal technology creates a new source of comparative advantage that changes the existing competition.**

That's testable.

That's a very clean INST contribution.

You aren't saying:

> Democracy is intrinsically better.

You're asking:

> **Can a fiscal technology create endogenous pressure toward the institutional conditions required to sustain it?**

That's much more interesting academically.

# 7. There is one important caveat

The existing literature **does not establish your full WDT-specific mechanism**.

The taxation → representation literature generally concerns taxation of citizens, often including VAT and income taxes. Kato & Tanaka actually point out that contemporary autocracies frequently adopt modern taxation **before** democratisation, meaning taxation isn't mechanically sufficient to cause democracy. ([Wiley Online Library][2])

That is actually useful for you.

Because the proposition shouldn't be:

> **Taxation causes democracy.**

It should be:

> **WDT may create unusually strong representation incentives because of the particular distribution of who pays, who benefits, and what institutional commitments are required to make the system credible.**

That distinction is critical.

### §5 — Conditions for a stable WDT equilibrium

Establish the four conditions exactly as you have them.

### §6 — The endogenous institutional consequence

Instead of immediately saying authoritarianism is excluded, ask:

> **What happens if an authoritarian government nevertheless attempts to satisfy the conditions?**

Then trace two constituencies:

**6.1 Taxpayer representation**

**6.2 Majority representation**

The labour dividend gives the non-taxpaying majority a direct material interest in the continuation of the fiscal architecture.

**6.3 Institutional feedback**

The two constituencies reinforce the same institutional direction.

**6.4 The developmental-authoritarian case**

This becomes the genuinely interesting case.

A developmental authoritarian government may initially be capable of adopting WDT-compatible institutions.

But the question becomes whether it can **stop the institutional process once the fiscal architecture has created constituencies with an interest in representation.**

That is much stronger than:

> “Authoritarian states can't implement WDT.”

## And there is a particularly nice implication

It gives you a potential distinction between **ordinary taxation and WDT**:

| Conventional taxation                                              | WDT                                                                                             |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Majority pays                                                      | Wealth holders primarily pay                                                                    |
| Majority demands representation because it is taxed                | Wealth holders demand credible protection                                                       |
| Government can spend revenue however it chooses                    | Revenue architecture is tied to refund + labour relief                                          |
| Benefits may be diffuse                                            | Labour dividend creates a visible benefit                                                       |
| Taxpayer coalition and beneficiary coalition overlap substantially | **Taxpayer and beneficiary coalitions are separated**                                           |
| Tax resistance can be politically divisive                         | **Taxpayer and beneficiary interests can potentially converge around institutional protection** |

That last point is potentially huge.

The wealthy taxpayer wants **credible protection and predictable rules**.

The majority wants **the labour-tax dividend**.

The WDT governance architecture is what connects the two.

So instead of:

> **taxation → resistance → democracy**

you potentially have:

> **taxpayer protection + majority fiscal benefit → shared interest in preserving the institutional architecture → representation.**

That is a much more distinctive hypothesis.

And it fits extremely well with your existing GOV/POL architecture, because your DR chamber and labour dividend weren't originally designed as *democratisation mechanisms*. They become something more interesting in INST: **institutions that may generate their own constituency for continued accountability.**
