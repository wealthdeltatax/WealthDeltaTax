# The Wealth Delta Tax: A Plain English Introduction

---

## The idea in one minute

Imagine three taxpayers already above the WDT threshold, each worth £100 million at the start of the year.

Alice is still worth £100 million at the end of the year. She pays nothing.
Ben is worth £120 million. He pays tax on the £20 million increase.
Charlie is worth £90 million. He receives a refund on part of the £10 million fall.

That is the Wealth Delta Tax. Under the current system, Ben could make that £20 million gain, never sell the asset, and pay no tax on it at all. Not this year, not next year, possibly never. The WDT exists because it treats that increase in wealth as economically real whether or not Ben chooses to sell.

Everything else in the proposal (the valuation architecture, the corporate instrument, the governance structure, the sovereign fund) exists to make that principle work fairly and reliably across the full range of assets wealthy people actually hold, and to protect the commitments it creates from being quietly abandoned over time.

This document explains what the proposal is, why it is built the way it is, and what it is not. It is written for a reader with no background in economics, tax, or law. The research papers that make up the full proposal, and a paper-by-paper summary, exist separately.

---

## What is broken about the current system

Wealthy people depend on everyone else to be wealthy. A founder whose company is worth £500 million did not build that value alone. They needed customers to buy, workers to produce, roads and courts and schools to function, and a stable society in which contracts are enforceable and property is protected. The wealth is real, and the effort behind it may be real, but it is also partly a collective product. It is built on the labour and consumption of millions of ordinary people, and on the public infrastructure their taxes fund. That is not a moral judgment about any individual. It is a structural fact about how large fortunes are built and sustained.

The current tax system does not reflect this dependency at all. If you earn a wage, you pay tax on it immediately, at source, before you see the money. There is no choice and no deferral. If your investment portfolio grows by the same amount, you often pay nothing. The tax is triggered only when you sell, and in some cases is eliminated entirely at death. The system treats identical economic enrichment completely differently depending on how it arrives.

As wealth has shifted away from wages and toward investment assets over recent decades, the gap has widened into something that is now difficult to defend on any principled basis. The tax burden increasingly falls on the people least able to avoid it, the same people whose labour and consumption the largest fortunes depend upon.

Tax systems evolved around administrative events (a sale, a dividend, an inheritance) because those were the moments at which a value could be observed and a bill could be sent. That was a practical constraint in an earlier era. It is now a structural choice with large distributional consequences, and one that nobody would design from scratch if starting today.

The most glaring example of this is a strategy sometimes called "buy, borrow, die." An investor holds an asset worth £10 million that has never been sold, so has never been taxed. Rather than selling, they borrow £10 million against it and live off the loan. Their net worth is unchanged (the new cash and the new debt cancel out) and no taxable event has occurred. The loan is repaid, if at all, from the estate after death, often at a stepped-up basis that eliminates the accumulated gain entirely. The asset appreciated for decades. The state saw none of it.

The WDT closes this automatically, without a special rule. Because the tax base is net worth (assets minus liabilities), borrowing against an asset is neutral by construction. Take out a £10 million loan against a £10 million asset and you have £10 million more in cash and £10 million more in debt. Your net worth has not changed. There is no delta. There is no tax. But there is also no advantage: the asset's appreciation has already been entering the tax base each year as it accrued. There is nothing left to defer by borrowing against it. The loophole closes not because a new rule targets it, but because the thing the loophole was exploiting (the gap between economic enrichment and taxable events) no longer exists.

Most people already accept that wages should be taxed. The question this proposal asks is why economically equivalent increases in wealth should often wait decades before being treated the same way.

## Why taxing changes rather than wealth itself matters

Traditional wealth taxes tax what you own. The WDT taxes only what you gained this year. That distinction is the foundation on which the rest of the proposal is built, and it changes the problem in several important ways.

A tax on the stock of wealth creates a bill every year regardless of whether anything actually happened. If your wealth is locked in a private company that did not change in value, you still owe tax. You may be forced to sell assets to pay it. You are penalised for holding rather than for gaining.

A tax on the annual change does not have this property. If your wealth did not grow, you owe nothing. The tax tracks enrichment rather than existence. This is also what makes the refund coherent: if the tax base is the change in your position, then a negative change is a legitimate basis for a refund on the same logic that a positive change is a legitimate basis for a tax.

The delta structure also changes how valuation works, in ways that matter a great deal. A declared value today becomes the starting point from which all future changes are measured. Understating your company's value does not eliminate a tax bill. It defers it, and the deferred amount grows as the company grows. This is explored further below.

## What the money is for

The WDT is designed to shift taxation away from labour and consumption and toward increases in wealth. The overall burden on the economy does not increase.

Most people in employment are already paying more in tax than they realise. Income tax and National Insurance are deducted before wages arrive. VAT adds 20% to most purchases. Fuel duty, alcohol and tobacco duties, and stamp duty on property transactions all take further shares at moments that feel like private choices rather than tax events. Mandatory pension contributions are not a tax in name, but they are a compulsory deduction from take-home pay. Add these up across a working life and the ordinary person's relationship with the state is one of continuous, highly visible extraction.

Under the proposal, WDT revenue is dedicated to reducing these burdens. The principal targets are income tax, National Insurance, and VAT, with the specific relief decisions made by the governance structure as the system matures and revenue becomes predictable. The people benefiting most from long-run wealth accumulation (the same people whose fortunes depend, as the opening of this document argues, on the labour, consumption, and public infrastructure of everyone else) would bear a greater share of the cost of maintaining the systems that made that accumulation possible.

An implementation that collected WDT revenue but spent it on something else would satisfy the fiscal mechanics while failing entirely on the proposal's own terms. The reduction in taxes on wages and consumption is not a secondary political consideration. It is the point.

Consider Sarah, who earns £38,000 a year. The WDT threshold is in the region of £5 million in net worth. She would need to save every penny of her salary for over a century to reach it. She will never be subject to the WDT, and she never pays it. But she currently pays income tax and National Insurance on every pound she earns, and VAT on most of what she spends. Under a mature WDT, those are the taxes that fall. Sarah's relationship with the proposal is entirely on the receiving end. The people above the threshold pay the WDT; Sarah gets the relief.

## Why this matters beyond tax

Although implemented through the tax system, the problem the WDT addresses is broader than taxation itself.

Tax systems influence who ultimately finances the state. Over time, those choices affect the relationship between economic power and political power. When the tax burden falls increasingly on wages and consumption while large increases in wealth go largely untaxed, the people generating that wealth face a different relationship with the state than the people whose labour underlies it.

When wealth concentrates beyond a certain point, it starts reshaping the institutional landscape around it. Democratic institutions become less responsive to the population at large and more responsive to concentrated private power. This is not a claim about bad intentions. It is a claim about structural mechanics: a democratic government with a short electoral mandate is structurally mismatched against long-duration concentrated capital. Money outlasts the political cycles that are supposed to check it.

The WDT's goal is a society in which substantial private wealth can exist without becoming large enough to undermine the institutions everyone depends upon. It tolerates and in some respects depends upon significant private wealth. What it does not tolerate is concentration so extreme that it begins to rewrite the rules in its own favour.

## What the WDT is not

A number of reasonable-sounding objections dissolve once it is clear what the WDT is not proposing.

**It is not a tax on existing wealth.** Wealth accumulated before the system comes into force is grandfathered. It establishes the baseline from which future changes are measured, but it is not itself taxed. The WDT taxes future accumulation, not the position you were already in.

**It is not trying to eliminate wealth concentration or flatten society.** The goal is not equality of outcome. Significant private wealth is tolerated and, in some respects, depended upon. The concern is narrower: that concentration beyond a certain point undermines the functioning of democratic institutions, and that a mechanism is therefore needed to maintain a sustainable equilibrium rather than to abolish accumulation.

**It is not a government takeover of private companies.** Wealthy individuals are assessed on the value of their own holdings (a stake in a company, not the company itself). Where someone holds a stake in a private company and settles their tax liability in kind by transferring a small proportional slice of equity rather than paying cash, the state does acquire that slice. This is a feature of the design rather than a bug: it avoids forcing cash sales of illiquid assets. But it means the state becomes a minority shareholder in some operating businesses over time. That is not the same as a takeover, and the design includes mechanisms for the state to exit those positions in an orderly way rather than holding them permanently. Separately, a last-resort option allows the state to acquire an asset at the taxpayer's own declared value in cases of dramatic understatement. This is intended to be rare, and its purpose is deterrence rather than acquisition.

**It is not designed to force asset sales.** A founder whose entire wealth is tied up in an illiquid private company stake is not expected to liquidate it each year to pay the tax. The design includes options for settling in kind, structured deferral, and a government-backed credit facility. The tax should not require you to destroy what it is taxing.

**It is not a replacement for all existing taxes on day one.** The proposal is explicit that a transition of this scale must be phased, incremental, and gated on conditions rather than a calendar. The first stage operates at a high threshold affecting a small number of people, while the infrastructure is built and the system is tested. The broader shift happens once it has been demonstrated to work.

## Why the refund exists

Most tax systems only take when things go well. If your portfolio crashes, the government does not share in that loss. It participated in your gains but offered nothing on the downside. The WDT treats this as a problem, and not just for strategic reasons. It is a matter of principle.

If the state is going to tax your gains when wealth grows, then on the same logic it should share in your losses when wealth falls. A government that participates only in upside is not a partner. It is a selective extractor. The refund is what the commitment to partnership actually requires.

There is also a practical argument. The people the WDT most needs to reach are also the people best positioned to resist it: they can move, restructure, and litigate in ways ordinary taxpayers cannot. A system that only ever takes, and offers nothing in bad years, gives them every rational incentive to escape it. A system that shares both gains and losses gives them a genuine reason to stay and participate. Whether that materially changes behaviour is an empirical question the project does not pretend to have answered. The narrower claim is that a system built on reciprocity is more likely to survive contact with its targets than one built on pure extraction.

---

## Why the fund exists

The refund creates a liability the government has to be able to honour. In a bad year (a market crash, a recession) wealth falls across the board. Refund obligations spike at precisely the moment public finances are already under pressure and borrowing conditions are difficult. Without a dedicated reserve, the government's options are to borrow more, suspend the refunds, or create money. All three undermine the mechanism.

The Sovereign Wealth Fund solves this by spreading the cost over time. In good years, when the tax collects more than is needed to run the system, the surplus goes into the fund. In bad years, the fund covers the refund obligations. The refunds are contractual, not discretionary. The fund exists because a future government facing a bad year must not be in a position to simply decide not to pay them. The fund is not a general government revenue pot. It exists for one purpose, and that restriction is built into the governance architecture rather than left to the goodwill of future governments.

The fund is analogous to a pension reserve (an institution that pre-funds a known future liability) rather than to the typical image of a sovereign wealth fund as an oil-revenue vehicle. The Norwegian and Chilean precedents are closer models than the Gulf funds.

## Why valuation is not an insurmountable problem

The standard objection to any annual wealth tax is that you cannot value a private company, a family farm, or a rare painting every year. Professional judgment involves discretion. Discretion produces a range. A range is not a tax bill.

This objection is aimed at the wrong target.

The WDT does not need annual precision. It needs declared values to carry real consequences. Because the WDT taxes the change in net worth rather than the stock, any value a taxpayer declares today becomes the recognised starting point for all future calculations. Declare your company too low today, and when it sells for more in ten years, the full gap between your declaration and the sale price enters the tax base in the year of sale. Understatement does not eliminate the tax. It defers it, and the deferred amount grows with the asset. Across most scenarios, honest declaration is simply the cheapest long-run strategy.

For hard-to-value assets (private company stakes, art, family farms) the taxpayer self-declares a value, and the delta mechanism is the primary reason that declaration is kept honest. Two further backstops sit on top of this: for company stakes, settling in kind by transferring a proportional equity slice means the declared value directly determines how much of the company changes hands; and as a last resort, the fund can acquire an asset at the price the taxpayer themselves declared, which makes extreme understatement dangerous in a direct and calculable way. These problems have been thought about. The detailed engineering is in the companion papers.

## What the WDT does not yet know

The proposal does not claim to have answered every question.

How much wealthy people will restructure their affairs, move abroad, or find avoidance strategies that reduce the effective tax base are empirical questions that only a live system can answer. The modelling uses the best available historical data and academic evidence, but the behavioural response of the specific population the WDT most needs to reach remains unknown in advance.

The international picture is incomplete. Wealth that moves offshore, or that is held through jurisdictions outside existing information-exchange frameworks, is harder to reach. This is a gap the system manages rather than closes, and it is named as such.

The transition from the current tax system to a WDT-funded one involves political and economic uncertainties that no model can fully capture. The proposal's answer to this is incremental implementation and honest sequencing, not a claim that the path is smooth.

These are real uncertainties, and the proposal names them rather than papering over them. But uncertainty about the scale of a problem is not the same as uncertainty about whether the problem exists. The structural gap in the current system is not in dispute. The question is whether to act on it or to continue treating historical administrative convenience as if it were principled design.

---

## Why uncertainty is not a reason to do nothing

Wealthy people already restructure their affairs and move assets offshore under the current system. The strategies described earlier in this document (borrowing against appreciating assets, holding wealth through offshore structures, deferring realisation indefinitely) are not hypothetical risks. They are standard practice. The question is not whether the WDT introduces avoidance behaviour but whether it produces more of it than a system that already rewards avoidance at every turn.

The claim that wealthy people will leave if taxed is the most common argument deployed against any proposal of this kind, and it deserves a direct response. Followed consistently, it makes wealth taxation permanently impossible: any rate above zero risks some departure, so the logical endpoint is a zero rate. That is not a policy conclusion. It is a veto dressed as an economic prediction.

The WDT takes the risk seriously. It is designed to measure actual behavioural response during a limited first phase before the system scales, and its cooperative architecture is partly designed to give the people best positioned to leave a reason to stay. But designing around a risk is different from accepting that the risk makes the attempt illegitimate.

The WDT's central claim is simple: economic enrichment should be taxed when it occurs, gains and losses should be treated symmetrically, and the proceeds should reduce the burden on the people whose wages and spending currently carry the greatest share. A system built on those three commitments, and designed to make them credible rather than merely aspirational, is worth attempting.
