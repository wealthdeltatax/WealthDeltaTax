## 4.5 Robustness: Revenue Target, Historical Windows, and Rate Parameters

The results in §§3–4.4 are all presented at the canonical parameters: E[T] = 2% of W₀, the 2000 start year, and the logistic rate function at τ₀ = 15%, τ_m = 70%, k = 0.001, W_min = £2m. Three questions test whether those results are artefacts of the specific parameter choices or structurally stable properties of the comparison.

First, do the rankings hold if the revenue target is materially higher or lower than 2% of W₀? Second, is the canonical 2000 start year representative of the historical experience — or is it an adverse outlier that flatters the WDT's symmetric refund mechanism by concentrating loss years in the scenario window? Third, are the three D-M complications in §4.1 genuinely second-order across the full logistic parameter space, or only at the canonical τ₀, τ_m, k, and W_min? Each question is addressed by an independent sweep in (WFR.A §E). The figures and prose results are reported here; the underlying simulation tables are in (WFR.A §E.1–§E.3.4).

### 4.5.1 Revenue Target Sensitivity

The revenue target E[T] = 2% of W₀ is the canonical choice throughout this paper, following the convention in (RATES) and (SWEEPS). Sweeping from 1% to 5% of W₀ tests whether the welfare rankings are stable as the aggregate revenue burden doubles or halves from the central case.

Table 12 reports CEW by system and revenue target at γ=2 for both distributions. Full tables are in (WFR.A §E.1).

|:---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Flat WDT | −0.871% | −1.754% | −2.650% | −4.485% |
| Income Tax | −0.881% | −1.771% | −2.672% | −4.503% |

| Stock Wealth Tax | −0.944% | −1.887% | −2.831% | −4.718% |

*Ver. A distribution. Revenue equivalence solved numerically at each target. Full five-target table in (WFR.A §E.1).*

No ranking reversal occurs across the full 1%–5% range. The flat WDT leads income tax and CGT at every target in Ver. A; stock wealth and consumption tax sit below all accrual-base systems at every target. The welfare costs scale approximately linearly with revenue extracted — the slope is nearly constant across systems — because the underlying mechanism in each case is proportional to the rate required to hit the revenue target. This linearity is not a constraint of the model: it follows from the revenue-equivalence design, which holds the relative burden proportionally constant as the target moves. A ranking that holds at 2% of W₀ therefore holds at any revenue target in the tested range without requiring the specific 2% choice.

The W₀-invariance result in Figure 4.5.1b confirms a related property. Fixing E[T] at 2% of W₀ and varying W₀ from £3m to £140m produces flat CEW series for every system: the welfare outcome is independent of initial wealth level under the revenue-equivalence design. This is the cleanest possible confirmation that the comparison is not sensitive to the specific wealth level at which the single-agent baseline is evaluated. The two-cluster structure from §3.1 is reproduced exactly across the full W₀ range.

### 4.5.2 Historical Window Robustness

The canonical scenario is a 30-year window starting in 2000, chosen because it is the most recent non-overlapping 30-year window in the JST dataset and because it encompasses both the dot-com correction and the global financial crisis — two of the most severe return sequences in the historical record. This makes the 2000 start year a conservative choice for evaluating the WDT, since its symmetric refund is most valuable precisely in windows with large negative-return episodes. Establishing that the WDT leads across the full set of historical windows — not only in windows that include severe losses — confirms that the §3.1 result is not an artefact of an adversely chosen starting point.

Sweep B applies the same revenue-equivalence comparison across all 73 possible 30-year windows in the dataset, from the 1947 start year (window 1947–1976) to the 2019 start year (window 2019–2029, completed with wrap-around to the beginning of the dataset). Table E.2.1 in (WFR.A) reports the full distribution of CEW outcomes.

The flat WDT achieves the highest CEW of any system in 100% of the 73 historical windows. Its median CEW across windows is −1.673%, against −1.675% for income tax and CGT and −1.814% for stock wealth and consumption tax. Its worst-case CEW (−1.765%, from the most adverse start year in the dataset) is higher than the worst-case CEW of any competing system. The WDT's median advantage over the stock wealth tax is 14.2 basis points — approximately the same as the canonical 2000-start result of 13.3 basis points, confirming that 2000 is not an outlier within the historical distribution.

Table E.2.2 in (WFR.A) reports the curated worst-case start years: 1972 (oil shock), 1987 (Black Monday window), 1999 and 2000 (GFC in window), and 2006 (worst LRR fill speed in the RATES analysis). The WDT advantage over the stock wealth tax ranges from 11.8 basis points (1972 start) to 14.6 basis points (2006 start). No adverse start year narrows the advantage below 11 basis points or produces a reversal.

The mechanism behind the WDT's cross-window stability is the same mechanism that explains its baseline advantage. Stock wealth and consumption tax CEW varies more across start years (spread from −1.731% to −1.894%) than WDT CEW (−1.587% to −1.765%), because the stock base collects its full levy regardless of return performance in each year of the window. A start year that happens to include more negative-return years reduces the stock base's revenue expectation slightly — producing the somewhat better outcomes in adverse windows — but does not provide any refund in those years, so the welfare cost of the negative-return states falls fully on the taxpayer. The WDT's symmetric refund moderates the worst outcomes without requiring a fortuitously timed start year.

### 4.5.3 Progressive Rate Parameter Sensitivity

Section 4.1 established that the three D-M complications — C1 (progression itself), C2 (the leverage interaction), C3 (the intertemporal rate asymmetry) — are all second-order at canonical logistic parameters, with C1 producing a flat-versus-progressive welfare gap of −0.00 basis points at the canonical test wealth. The claim that C1 is second-order is quantitatively specific: it depends on the logistic being near-flat at the tested wealth levels relative to the canonical τ₀, τ_m, k, and W_min. Sweep C varies each of those four parameters independently, holding the others at canonical values, to test whether the C1 conclusion is fragile to parameter choice.

The four sweeps are reported in full in (WFR.A §E.3.1–§E.3.4). The results across all four axes are the same: the flat-versus-progressive welfare gap (Gap = CEW_flat − CEW_progressive, basis points) remains below 0.05 basis points in absolute magnitude across the full tested range for every wealth level and every parameter combination, with one exception.

The exception is the W_min sweep at W₀ = £10m. When W_min is raised to £10m — equal to W₀ — the agent sits exactly at the entry threshold, the progressive schedule applies only the entry rate τ₀, and the gap turns positive (+1.74 basis points, meaning the flat WDT has marginally higher welfare cost than the progressive). This is not an economically meaningful exception. It requires W_min ≈ W₀, which means the taxpayer is simultaneously being assessed and sitting exactly at the threshold below which they would not be assessed at all. It is a boundary condition of the parameterisation rather than a parameter combination the policy would ever operate at. No other combination in the four sweeps produces a positive gap or a gap approaching 0.1 basis points in absolute value.

The steepness parameter k produces the largest absolute gaps within the feasible range: at k = 0.01 and W₀ = £100m, the gap reaches −0.05 basis points. This is still two orders of magnitude smaller than the baseline welfare differences between systems in §3.1, confirming that even an aggressively steep logistic — one well outside the canonical calibration — does not produce a C1 complication large enough to alter the comparative welfare picture. The crossing of the £100m series at k = 0.05 (where the gap turns positive) reflects the agent reaching the region above the logistic inflection point, where gain and refund rates reconverge near the τ_m ceiling. That crossing occurs far outside the canonical parameter space and is noted for completeness rather than as a policy-relevant result.

Taken together, the four sweeps confirm what the §4.1 analysis established for the canonical case: the logistic progressive schedule does not produce D-M complications of policy-relevant magnitude across the full tested parameter space. The second-order conclusion does not rest on a fortuitous choice of canonical parameters.
