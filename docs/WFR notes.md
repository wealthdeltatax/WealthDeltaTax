Having actually run every module, fixed the bugs in the table pipeline, and cross-checked every reproducible number against the canon appendix, I have more hands-on confidence in the *arithmetic* than I would from reading alone. But "does the argument hold" is a different, higher bar than "is the arithmetic right" — and I want to separate those two things clearly rather than let verified numbers imply a verified argument.

## Short answer

As a **Level-1 theoretical/structural paper** — which is exactly how it describes itself — it's genuinely well-built: unusually disciplined about revenue equivalence, unusually honest about its own limitations, and the numbers check out. As a **standalone policy argument** — which the abstract and §6.3 occasionally drift toward sounding like — it doesn't yet hold on its own, and I don't think it's supposed to; it's explicitly deferring the load-bearing parts to EVAL and PHASE1. The gap between those two framings is the main thing I'd push on.

## What's genuinely strong

- **Revenue equivalence as the organizing discipline.** Solving for τ* rather than comparing systems at arbitrary rates is the single choice that makes everything downstream defensible. Most popular wealth-tax comparisons don't do this and it shows.
- **The sequential admission of distortions (Part I baseline → Part II mechanisms)** is a real identification strategy, not just narrative structure. Because Part I shows the systems are nearly tied (17.4bp) with distortions suppressed, every large number in Part II can be attributed to a *named* mechanism rather than to bundled effects. That's the paper's best methodological feature.
- **The Category 1/2/3 taxonomy** is good epistemic hygiene and it's applied consistently, not just stated once and forgotten — Table 11's literature map holds itself to it row by row.
- **Willingness to report counterintuitive or inconvenient results.** Table 4's negative "Excess" column (progression collecting *less* than flat, contrary to textbook bracket intuition) gets explained rather than smoothed over. The Corner B "—" cell (no feasible flat rate) gets a real explanation rather than being dropped. That's the right instinct.
- **§2.4's disclosure of comparable-treatment asymmetry** — stating outright that CGT is tested with one real distortion admitted while WDT is tested with zero implementation costs admitted — is honest in a way a lot of advocacy-adjacent papers wouldn't be.

## Where I'd push back if I were refereeing this

**1. The comparable-treatment asymmetry is disclosed but not neutralized, and §6.3 leans on it anyway.** §2.4 tells the reader plainly that the comparison structurally favors WDT (one side accumulates a real distortion, the other doesn't by design). That's fine as a disclosed scope limitation. But then §6.3's "burden-of-consideration" framing argues from the 141–143bp lock-in advantage as though it's a clean welfare finding, when it's really "WDT's best case vs. CGT's one identified flaw." The paper's own answer — EVAL will do the break-even analysis — is a reasonable deferral, but it means the strong rhetorical framing ("cannot reasonably be dismissed") is currently unearned by this paper alone. I'd want that softened until EVAL exists.

**2. The concentration numbers are more mechanical than the framing suggests.** 479× vs 286–288× (and now 3,173× vs 178× at N=73) get repeated in the abstract, §4.3, §6.1, and §6.3 — they're clearly meant to be the headline result. But holding a fixed, non-mean-reverting 8pp return differential constant for 30 or 73 years and then observing that a return-conditioned tax base attenuates the compounding better than a stock base is close to an algebraic consequence of the setup, not really a discovery. Fagereng et al. document *persistence*, not permanence — real individual return differentials aren't invariant across seven decades. This is eventually caveated ("model-simulated," §6.1.1) but not at the point where the numbers first land in the abstract, which is where a skeptical reader forms their anchor.

**3. The N=73 extension I just helped build surfaces something worth investigating, not just reporting.** Progressive WDT's Great/Poor ratio isn't monotonic over 1947–2019 — it peaks around 1977 (~222×), falls to a trough near 146× through the 1990s, then climbs back to 178× by 2019. Neither the table notes nor my own figure captions actually explain *why*. My working suspicion — untested — is that this is at least partly a denominator-stability artifact: the Poor tier's wealth trajectory under a persistent −4.55pp differential against a 10.45% mean is compounding slowly and could be passing through low-wealth periods where the ratio is numerically sensitive to small denominator moves, rather than the swings being a meaningful statement about tax policy. I'd want that checked — plotting the Poor tier's raw wealth path over 1947–2019 alongside the ratio — before this figure goes in front of a referee who will ask the same question.

**4. Sweep B's "100% of windows" framing overstates independence.** All 73 start-year windows are drawn from overlapping 30-year slices of the *same* 73-year series — adjacent windows share 29 of 30 years. That's not 73 independent robustness checks; it's closer to one time series examined from many angles. The claim itself (WDT best in every window) is true as stated, but "100% of windows" reads to an unwary audience like a stronger statistical statement than 73 heavily autocorrelated samples actually license. Worth a one-line caveat.

**5. The D-M "confirmed to floating-point precision" result is a unit test dressed as an empirical finding.** A flat symmetric tax satisfying Domar–Musgrave to 10⁻¹⁶ precision is guaranteed by construction — the code implements exactly the D-M assumptions. §3.2 does eventually say "mechanism established, verdict deferred," which is the right instinct, but the amount of table and figure real estate spent on it (Table 2, Figure 3.2, the variance table) slightly overstates its evidentiary weight relative to what it actually shows, which is "the code correctly implements the formula."

**6. Wealth is modeled as 100% UK equity exposure throughout, and this is easy to miss.** Every module — Modules 1 through 5, all four wealth tiers — uses the same 73-year UK equity return series as the entire return process. Real taxpayer wealth includes bonds, property, private business equity, and much more. This is a substantial simplification for a paper whose central claims are about how a *wealth* tax behaves, and it's mentioned only implicitly (via "JST dataset," "UK equity") rather than flagged as a scope limitation the way the pre-behavioural assumption is in §7.1.

**7. The Guvenen vs. Boadway–Spiritus non-adjudication (§5.5) is honest, but it's the elephant in the room, not a side issue.** The paper's central instrument is exactly the kind of return-conditioned base that dispute is about. Declining to adjudicate is correct given the partial-equilibrium scope, but it means the welfare ranking here could in principle reverse under the general-equilibrium channel Boadway–Spiritus emphasize. §7.2 says this plainly, but it's forty pages downstream of an abstract that reads more confidently than that caveat would suggest is warranted.

## What I can vouch for with actual confidence, vs. what I can't

Having run the pipeline end to end and diffed every reproducible table against the canon, I can say with real confidence: **the arithmetic is internally consistent, the code implements what the prose says it implements, and the numbers in the appendix are trustworthy given the model's own assumptions.** That's not nothing — I found and fixed real labeling bugs (mislabeled distribution columns, a wrong reference window in the new Part F) that would have embarrassed the paper if a referee had spotted them first.

What I can't vouch for, because it's not a code-correctness question, is whether the *assumptions* — fixed non-mean-reverting return differentials, equities-only wealth, pre-behavioural agents, the asymmetric distortion-admission design — are the right ones for the conclusions being drawn. That's an economics judgment call, and it's the one I'd want a second, adversarial read on before treating the headline numbers as settled.

## Bottom line

It holds its argument for the paper it says it is. It doesn't yet hold the argument the abstract sometimes sounds like it's making. The honest move — which the paper mostly already makes, just not always at full volume — is to keep saying "Level 1, pre-behavioural, partial equilibrium, deferred to EVAL" every time a headline number appears, not just in §7.
